I · Opening hand

The One-Drop That Played Every Stage of the Game

Original object · Return to Ravnica

Deathrite Shaman

Creature — Elf Shaman

{T}: Exile target land card from a graveyard. Add one mana of any color. {B}, {T}: Exile target instant or sorcery card from a graveyard. Each opponent loses 2 life. {G}, {T}: Exile target creature card from a graveyard. You gain 2 life.

Original setReturn to Ravnica · 2012
Mana cost{B/G}
Mana value1.0
Power / toughness1 / 2

Deathrite Shaman entered Return to Ravnica disguised as a utility creature. Its body was only 1/2, each ability required a card in a graveyard, and two of the three activations asked for colors different from its hybrid casting cost. On the table, those restrictions proved less like weaknesses than a menu.

The first ability converted fetch lands into mana. The second turned spent instants and sorceries into two-point life swings. The third exiled creatures to gain life. Because the Shaman could use either graveyard, every normal exchange in a game—fetching, casting removal, trading creatures—stocked its future activations.

That breadth made the card unusually hard to age out of a game. Early, it jumped from one mana to three. In the middle turns, it fixed colors and denied recursion. Late, it became repeatable damage that ignored blockers. Modern Jund and Legacy blue-black-green decks did not have to choose between mana acceleration and a live topdeck; the same card supplied both.

The result was a rare policy story. Modern banned Deathrite Shaman in February 2014 because black-green attrition decks could accelerate without sacrificing late-game strength. Legacy followed in July 2018 after Grixis Delver and other Shaman decks compressed the format around the same one-drop.

II · Origin

Ravnica put a shaman between the guilds

Return to Ravnica brought the Golgari back as the guild of rot, recycling, and graveyard value. Deathrite Shaman expresses that identity without using the set’s marquee scavenge mechanic: it treats dead cards as fuel and converts each card type into a different resource.

The hybrid black-green cost suggested accessibility, but the activation costs quietly invited more colors. A deck could cast the Shaman from a black source, use fetch lands to make green mana, then splash blue or red while retaining the black drain ability. That interaction helped the card escape the boundaries of a conventional Golgari deck.

Steve Argyle’s original illustration depicts the Shaman not as a battlefield champion but as a ritual specialist handling remains. The art makes the rules legible: corpses and residue are not background scenery; they are the inventory from which the card works.

Steve Argyle’s original Return to Ravnica artworkThe illustration organizes the figure through a readable silhouette, layered depth, and directional light. Foreground texture and background atmosphere keep the subject legible at card size while still suggesting a larger world beyond the frame.
III · Rules

Three abilities, three different traps

The mana-producing ability targets a land card in a graveyard. Because it targets, it is not a mana ability under the rules and uses the stack. An opponent can exile the land in response, causing the ability to fail for lack of a legal target.

Summoning sickness matters because all three abilities use the tap symbol. A newly cast Shaman cannot immediately make mana unless it has haste. This makes turn-one survival the critical threshold: untapping once often repays the entire mana investment.

The drain ability does not deal damage. Each opponent loses two life, so prevention and effects that care about damage do not stop it. In multiplayer, one activation hits every opponent, which is why the card scales differently in Commander than in duel formats.

Every ability may target a card in any graveyard. This creates constant priority contests around fetch lands, flashback spells, reanimation targets, and delve fuel. The controller is not only spending a graveyard card; they are deciding which future resource the opponent no longer receives.

IV · Resource economy

The fifth mode was denying the opponent

Counting the printed modes understates the card. Exiling a land for mana may also prevent Life from the Loam; exiling an instant may shrink Tarmogoyf or deny delve; exiling a creature may interrupt reanimation. The Shaman’s output and its disruption occur in the same activation.

This is why normal one-for-one answers often felt late. If the Shaman produced mana once, drained once, or invalidated a graveyard card before dying, it had already changed the exchange. The opponent needed cheap removal at the correct moment rather than merely an answer somewhere in the deck.

The 1/2 body also mattered. It survived common one-damage effects, blocked many one-power creatures, and could carry Equipment. None of those jobs justified a card alone, but each raised the floor of a permanent already doing several more important things.

V · Tournament history

From Return to Ravnica to two banned lists

At Pro Tour Return to Ravnica in October 2012, Yuuya Watanabe’s Jund deck used four copies immediately. The card accelerated Liliana of the Veil and Bloodbraid Elf, fixed a demanding mana base, and gave the attrition deck reach after both players had traded resources.

By the 2013 World Championship, black-green Modern decks could be built around Deathrite Shaman, Tarmogoyf, discard, removal, and planeswalkers. Wizards’ February 2014 announcement identified the central problem: the format’s best attrition strategy received acceleration without accepting the normal late-game weakness of mana creatures.

Legacy magnified the card because fetch lands, Wasteland, Brainstorm, Daze, and efficient graveyard cards were already universal. BUG Delver, Shardless BUG, four-color control, and Grixis Delver all converted the Shaman into mana fixing and inevitability.

The July 2018 Legacy ban cited sustained popularity, strong non-mirror performance, reduced diversity, and hostility to rogue strategies. Daniel Duterte’s Grand Prix Seattle victory three months earlier stands as a final major snapshot of the Grixis Delver shell the announcement described.

VI · Construction

A mana creature that demanded graveyards, not forests

Deathrite Shaman is strongest when the deck can put lands into graveyards reliably. Fetch lands are the cleanest support because they make the first ability function without spending a spell. Without that density, the card becomes an inconsistent accelerator.

The black and green activations ask different questions. Black turns cheap spells into a clock; green stabilizes races and attacks creature recursion. Decks with both colors gain the full menu, while four-color Legacy lists often cast the Shaman from one color and used it to bridge into the others.

Because the card already interacts with graveyards, decks could reduce dedicated hate—but only to a point. A single activation per turn cannot replace broad answers to explosive graveyard combo. The Shaman was best as flexible pressure that complemented, rather than fully replaced, specialized sideboard cards.

VII · Sequencing

The best target is often the card the opponent needs next

On the first upkeep after casting Deathrite Shaman, identify which land can safely be consumed. Exiling your own fetch land may make mana now but remove a future target for Snapcaster Mage, delve, or recursion. Using the opponent’s land preserves your resources but may expose the activation to their graveyard response.

Against a spell-based deck, hold the black activation until the end step when possible. The threat of exiling an instant or sorcery can constrain flashback and delve throughout the turn. Activating too early tells the opponent which graveyard cards remain safe.

Against creature decks, the green mode is often a combat spell in disguise. Two life can change a race, but using the creature card before combat may also remove a reanimation target or reduce opposing delirium. Sequence the activation around both life totals and graveyard synergies.

When several Shamans are present, do not assume every activation has fuel. Each target is exiled on resolution, and a second activation targeting the same card will fail. Assign distinct targets before passing priority.

VIII · Counterplay

Remove it before the first untap—or attack its fuel

The highest-value answer is cheap removal before the Shaman untaps. A one-mana removal spell on turn one prevents the acceleration that makes later exchanges unfavorable. Waiting until the three-drop resolves may be strategically correct, but the opponent has already received the tempo.

Graveyard exile can counter individual activations because the abilities target. A fetch land may be sacrificed in response to create a replacement mana target, while an instant-speed exile effect can remove the chosen card and strand the ability.

Mana denial works differently than against ordinary mana creatures. Killing fetch lands with effects such as Stifle, limiting graveyards, or forcing the Shaman player to exile their own resources can reduce future utility even when the creature survives.

Finally, pressure the life total. A Shaman that must gain life cannot drain, and a Shaman that must block cannot tap. Fast creature decks sometimes beat the card by forcing its controller to choose only one of its many modes each turn.

IX · Format migration

Legal in some rooms, defining by absence in others

Deathrite Shaman is banned in Modern and Legacy as of this review. Its absence is part of both formats’ identity: Modern black-green decks must choose other accelerants or accept a slower curve, while Legacy tempo decks can no longer fix four colors and create inevitability from a single one-drop.

Vintage can still use the card, where restricted power and faster engines keep it from defining the entire format. It remains attractive in fair Sultai shells because graveyards, fetch lands, and Wasteland are plentiful.

Commander turns the black ability into multiplayer reach because each opponent loses life. The card is less reliable as pure acceleration in singleton decks without enough fetch lands, but its flexibility remains exceptional.

Pioneer begins with Return to Ravnica, so Deathrite Shaman is legal there. Its impact is much smaller because Pioneer’s fetch lands are banned and the format supplies fewer effortless land cards to exile, demonstrating how strongly the card’s power depended on its environment.

X · Art and memory

The one-mana planeswalker nickname was not entirely a joke

Players often called Deathrite Shaman a one-mana planeswalker. The nickname was imprecise but revealing: the card offered several repeatable abilities, influenced multiple zones, and changed its role as the game developed.

The card also became a symbol of “good-stuff” deck construction. It rewarded playing fetch lands, efficient interaction, and individually strong threats—the exact infrastructure already favored in eternal formats. Rather than ask for a narrow combo, it made the default powerful cards work better together.

Its bans therefore felt different from bans aimed at a single explosive engine. Deathrite Shaman was removed because it made fair decks too complete, not because it consistently produced one spectacular turn.

XI · Design echo

A safer mana creature kept one late-game promise

Delighted Halfling revisits the idea that a one-mana accelerator should remain relevant after the opening turns.

A comparative echo

Delighted Halfling

It fixes broadly for legendary spells and can make those spells uncounterable, but it does not attack graveyards, drain life, or produce unrestricted colored mana from a stocked graveyard.

The comparison shows how later designs preserved role compression while placing firmer boundaries around the second role.

XII · Decision point

Mana, damage, or denial?

A Legacy player controls Deathrite Shaman with one untapped land. The opponent has a fetch land, an instant, and a creature in the graveyard. The controller can make mana for a two-drop, drain for two, or gain life while denying reanimation.

The best activation depends on the next turn rather than the largest immediate number. Making mana may establish a threat that ends the game faster than one drain. Exiling the instant may turn off delve or Snapcaster Mage. Exiling the creature may be worth more than four life if it breaks the opponent’s engine.

This repeated decision is the card’s real power. Most one-drops ask whether they survive. Deathrite Shaman asks what resource the game will be about next.

XIII · Legacy

Efficiency became role compression

Deathrite Shaman taught designers and players that a mana creature’s late-game text can matter as much as its acceleration. Later one-drops often carry a second job, but they are usually restricted by creature type, spell type, legendary status, or narrower mana production.

The card’s history also demonstrates that flexibility can be more dangerous than raw speed. None of its activations looks unbeatable in isolation. Combining them on one cheap permanent removed too many deck-building costs.

Its lasting presence is paradoxical: two formats remember Deathrite Shaman most clearly through the deck-building space created when it left.
