I · The central bargain

The Land That Joined the Attack

Mishra's Factory lets a land do its ordinary work until combat offers something better. Pay one mana and it becomes a 2/2 artifact creature for the turn, still a land; leave it alone and it keeps making colorless mana. Its third ability turns another decision into a possible advantage: an untapped Factory can strengthen an Assembly-Worker, including itself after animation.

That combination made the card more than spare damage in a mana base. A control deck could keep a threat outside creature removal's reach between turns. A creature deck could add pressure after spending its hand. Two Factories could cooperate in combat, but only if their controller preserved the mana and untapped land needed for the line.

The important bargain is therefore visible at the table. Every animation spends mana and exposes a land. Every Factory left upright may be a mana source, a blocker, or a pump. The card's history lies in deciding which of those jobs is worth the commitment on this particular turn.

The card

Mishra's Factory

Land

{T}: Add {C}. {1}: Mishra's Factory becomes a 2/2 Assembly-Worker artifact creature until end of turn. It's still a land. {T}: Target Assembly-Worker creature gets +1/+1 until end of turn.

A story from the table

The blocker tapped after it blocked

My opponent attacked with Kird Ape, its Taiga keeping it at 2/3. My Mishra's Factory had been on the battlefield since my previous turn. Before blockers I tapped Swamp to animate it, then put the 2/2 Assembly-Worker in front of the Ape. After the block was declared, I tapped the Factory to give itself +1/+1. It was still blocking, and its three power still counted for combat damage under the rules we were using. The Ape took three, the Factory took two, and only the Ape went to the graveyard.

Strategic legacy

Why Mishra's Factory matters

Mishra’s Factory solved a control deck’s oldest tension by being both a land and a threat, then added combat depth through the ability of Assembly-Workers to reinforce one another.

How to use it

Develop mana first and animate only when the attack, block, or pump changes the exchange. Multiple Factories reward careful ordering because an unanimated copy can strengthen the one already in combat.

How to fight it

Use instant-speed removal after animation, land destruction before combat, or blockers that force the controller to risk a mana source. Sorcery-speed creature removal is often poorly positioned against it.

Tournament and cultural record

Andrew Raichlin's black-white deck won the twelve-player Card and Board Old School event on October 11, 2015. The organizer's published registration provides one concrete example of Factory supporting creatures, disruption, and white removal.

II · Origin

A factory in four seasons

Antiquities placed artifacts and lands inside the same industrial history. Mishra's Factory crossed that boundary in its rules: the land could become an artifact creature without ceasing to be a land. An ordinary mana source could therefore join combat, survive as infrastructure when the attack was unnecessary, and disappear from creature removal's reach when the turn ended.

The original four seasonal illustrations give that transformation an unusually tangible setting. This is a site that continues to exist while the game changes around it. The battlefield worker is temporary; the land remains. That distinction matters more than the shorthand ‘creature land’ suggests, because the card is not an artifact or a creature while its animation effect is absent.

The design also divides its abilities between three jobs. Producing colorless mana develops the game. Paying one mana creates a 2/2. Tapping the land can strengthen an Assembly-Worker. Each mode competes with the others, so the card's flexibility never means that every option is available at once.

Kaja Foglio & Phil Foglio’s original Antiquities artworkThe composition gives Mishra's Factory unusual scale through a muted palette, layered distance, and a strong silhouette. Rather than serving as a backdrop, the landscape feels ancient, watchful, and complete without any figure at its center.
III · Rules texture

The land remembers how long it has been there

Animating Mishra's Factory does not create a new permanent. A copy controlled since the beginning of its controller's most recent turn can attack after animation, even if it became a creature only moments ago. A copy played this turn cannot attack without haste. The relevant history is control of the permanent, not the number of turns spent as a creature.

That rule also governs its tap abilities while it is a creature. A newly played Factory can tap for mana while it is only a land. If it is animated first, summoning sickness prevents its mana and pump abilities from being activated that turn unless it has haste. The animation ability itself has no tap symbol in its cost, so activating that ability remains possible.

Paying for animation with the Factory's own mana is legal: tap it for colorless, then spend that mana on the activation. The resulting creature is tapped. It cannot attack or be declared as a blocker without another effect untapping it. This is why the familiar attacking line normally uses a separate mana source.

IV · Tournament evolution

A registered card, not only a design ancestor

Factory's tournament history is visible in registrations rather than in a single story of invention. The preserved Deck Archive includes early Constructed decks and later Old School lists. Those records show a land working inside aggressive and controlling shells, rather than requiring every deck to share one creature plan.

One directly documented example is Andrew Raichlin's black-white deck, which won the twelve-player Card and Board Old School tournament on October 11, 2015. Eternal Central published the organizer's results and deck photographs. The Factory belonged beside black creatures, disruption, and white removal; it did not need a dedicated artifact engine to contribute.

A different document, Alessandro Galli's published UW Landstill registration for a February 2026 Premodern event, lists four Factories and three Standstills. That is evidence of the pairing in an actual submitted list, not proof that every Landstill deck used the same numbers or achieved the same result. Its attraction is mechanical: land drops and Factory activations do not cast spells.

V · The field

One land, several distinct jobs

In an Old School creature deck, a Factory can turn an otherwise quiet mana draw into another attacker. In a slower black-white shell, it can wait behind removal and join combat after the opponent has spent answers on Hypnotic Specter or Juzám Djinn. Its colored-mana cost is indirect: every Factory is a land that cannot normally cast those black spells by itself.

In Landstill, the relationship with Standstill is more specific. Playing a land and activating an ability do not trigger the enchantment. A Factory already able to attack can therefore pressure the opponent without handing over three cards. The opponent may eventually need to cast a spell, but an opposing creature land or a superior existing battlefield can spoil that arrangement.

In a deck with Nevinyrral's Disk, the Factory's types determine whether it survives the reset. An unanimated copy is only a land and survives the Disk. An animated copy is an artifact creature and is destroyed with the other artifacts and creatures. The card protects a future attacker from a sweeper only when the pilot preserves the correct state.

VI · Technical play

Two Factories are a small combat system

A Factory attacking as a 2/2 is tapped from attacking, so it cannot normally tap to pump itself. A second, unanimated Factory can provide the bonus instead. That second land must remain untapped, and another source must pay for the attacker's animation. Counting both the payment and the lands committed to combat prevents the supposed free attack from consuming the mana intended for an answer.

Defense changes the calculation. Animate an older Factory with another source, declare it as a blocker while untapped, and then tap it to target itself with the pump ability. It becomes a 3/3. Under modern combat rules, tapping the blocker does not remove it from combat or stop its combat damage. Wizards' Modern Horizons 2 release notes give this exact line.

A newly played, unanimated Factory can also pump another Assembly-Worker. It is not a creature at that point, so the creature rule restricting tap abilities does not apply to it. Animating that new copy before trying to pump would change the answer. The order of those two decisions matters even though neither card's printed text changes.

VII · Failure modes

The attack spends more than one mana

The printed activation costs one mana. An attack also commits the Factory itself, which might otherwise have produced mana. A pump from a second copy commits another land. These are different costs, and confusing them leads to hands that can either attack or hold interaction, but cannot do both.

The colorless source is another real price. A hand with two Factories and Counterspell has lands but no blue mana. A black deck trying to cast Sinkhole faces the same problem in a different color. Increasing the number of Factory copies should follow a count of colored requirements, not the assumption that a useful land can replace any other land without consequence.

Animation also creates new vulnerabilities. Swords to Plowshares can exile the creature and permanently remove the mana source. Lightning Bolt kills a lone 2/2, and one self-pump only makes it a 3/3, still within Bolt range. A second untapped Factory can add another toughness, but that rescue also depends on which copies are available and what has already been tapped.

Against a large attacker, spending mana on a Factory block may buy only a turn. Against trample, the amount prevented depends on the actual blocker and damage assignment. Against a fast combo deck, turning a land into a small attacker may be far less important than preserving mana for disruption. The card's flexibility is useful because it permits restraint, not because it makes animation mandatory.

VIII · Counterplay

Wait for the land to expose another type

Creature removal often cannot touch an unanimated Factory. Waiting for animation opens that window, and destroying or exiling the creature also removes the land. An opponent can respond to a pump activation, to the animation having resolved, or to the beginning of combat before attackers are declared. Which window is best depends on the mana the Factory player has already spent.

Land destruction approaches the problem directly. Wasteland can target the nonbasic even while it is not a creature; Strip Mine can do the same. The Factory's controller may activate or tap it in response when the rules permit, but animation does not protect it from an ability that targets a land. It remains a land throughout the turn.

Artifact removal is conditional in a different way. Disenchant can target an animated Factory because it is an artifact then. It cannot target a normal, unanimated copy merely because the land's name sounds industrial. That distinction explains why the question ‘Is it animated?’ is a practical interaction check.

Forcing action can be as effective as saving a dedicated answer. An attack may compel a defensive animation before the Factory player wanted to spend mana. A removal spell aimed at that blocker can then open the battlefield while reducing the available mana for the next turn. The defending player must weigh the land against the life that blocking would save.

IX · Close play

The Disk turn has two different answers

Consider a later turn with an untapped Nevinyrral's Disk, a Factory controlled since the previous turn, and two Swamps available. If the goal is to clear the battlefield and then attack, spend one Swamp's mana to activate the Disk while the Factory is still only a land. The Factory survives the destruction. Use the other Swamp to animate it for combat.

Animating first changes the outcome. Even if the Factory remains untapped and no attack has been declared, it is now both an artifact and a creature. The Disk destroys it. Paying for animation again does not undo the type change; the effect still lasts until the turn ends.

If the opponent has left a creature on the other side, the attack also depends on what survives the reset and what can be cast afterward. This is a sequencing example, not a guarantee that one land wins any cleared board. It explains the specific advantage the Factory offers: the pilot can choose to postpone its creature status until after a sweeper that would otherwise kill it.

X · Format migration

Landstill made not casting a spell productive

Standstill changes the value of an unused turn. A player who casts the next spell can give the opponent three cards, but the enchantment does not stop land drops or activated abilities. Factory supplies an action that advances a win condition through precisely that gap.

The pairing is strongest when the Factory player already has a favorable battlefield. Casting Standstill while facing a larger creature is not a lock simply because a Factory is available. The pilot still needs to block, trade, find removal, or accept that breaking the enchantment may be necessary. An opposing Factory creates another fight that can take place without either player casting a spell.

That makes copy counts and utility lands part of the control plan. The Galli registration is one concrete example, with four Factories and two Faerie Conclaves among its lands. The list demonstrates redundant ways to act under Standstill; it does not imply that every format or every control deck can afford that many lands without ordinary colored output.

XI · Modern reading

Compare the job before choosing the land

Factory's competition includes lands that attack for more damage, produce colored mana, fly, carry useful creature types, or enter with another ability. A comparison should begin with the deck's actual task: cast its spells reliably, defend an early turn, attack under Standstill, or keep a threat after removal has traded away the creatures.

Mutavault preserves the one-mana animation into a 2/2, but becomes every creature type rather than an artifact creature with Factory's pump ability. That can matter for tribal bonuses. Factory instead offers its own Assembly-Worker support. Neither card removes a colored activation cost from the other; both have a generic one-mana activation.

In a historical environment, the original card pool is part of the reason to choose Factory. In a contemporary deck, its name is no substitute for a colored-source count or a matchup plan. Evaluate how often the land actually attacks, how often it blocks profitably, and how often its colorless output prevents the spell the hand needed to cast.

XII · Design and memory

The Foundry changed the defensive bargain

Mishra's Foundry makes the comparison unusually explicit. In his December 2022 Brothers' War mailbag, Mark Rosewater explained that the original Factory was too strong for the Standard environment and that the Foundry's pump was directed toward attacking Assembly-Workers to emphasize offense. The older card's ability to become a 3/3 blocker was the behavior being restrained.

That explanation identifies something more precise than an inevitable history of larger creatures. A land can be powerful because it discourages attacks while still occupying a mana slot. Removing the easy defensive pump changes when the opponent can profitably move to combat, even if the printed animated body still begins at 2/2.

Factory's design inheritance therefore includes two separate ideas: a mana base can supply a threat, and several copies of a land can cooperate through a creature type. Later designs can keep the first while changing the second. The original remains distinctive because its small numerical bonus sits at an important boundary in combat.

XIII · Testimony

The land left upright beside the others

The familiar table gesture is simple: one Factory is turned sideways with the attackers, another is left upright beside the lands. The second copy may represent mana, a pump, or a future creature. Both players have to decide which possibility is most important before an ability is activated.

The seasonal Antiquities illustrations add another kind of recognition. Four pictures can represent the same rules object without requiring four different cards or four different strategies. The art changes the season; the player still announces animation, counts the available mana, and asks whether the other Factory is staying back.

Those details are more specific than the claim that an old card once felt powerful. Factory makes the mana base visibly participate in combat. A player can lose a land to removal, preserve it through a sweeper, or decline to animate because the threat of a blocker is sufficient. The memory belongs to those decisions and to the small patch of untapped cardboard that keeps them available.

XIV · Design echo

Mutavault keeps the cost and changes the workforce

Mutavault and Factory both pay one generic mana to become a 2/2 until end of turn. Mutavault gains all creature types, which means an animated copy is also an Assembly-Worker and can receive a Factory's +1/+1 bonus. It does not acquire Factory's activated abilities, and being every creature type does not make it an artifact.

The distinction supports different decks. Mutavault can exploit a tribal bonus where its changing creature type is the point. Factory can provide a defensive self-pump and reinforce another Assembly-Worker without animating the supporting copy. The shared animation cost makes the difference in the rest of the text easier to see.

A later design echo

The same cost, a different creature type

Mutavault keeps the one-mana activation into a 2/2 and gains every creature type. Factory instead supplies the Assembly-Worker pump; an animated Mutavault can receive that bonus too.

The comparison reveals which part of Mishra's Factory design considered essential and which part later sets needed to restrain.
