I · The card

One Loyalty Turned Every Wasteland Into a Loop

Wrenn and Six belongs in the Honorable Mention archive because a two-mana planeswalker converted fetchlands and Wasteland into repeatable resources while threatening to invalidate one-toughness creatures.

Temur Delver and four-color shells used the card to turn mana denial into inevitability until its Legacy ban in 2019. Its prominence came from a precise fit between text, mana, and the unusual pressures of Legacy rather than from raw rate alone.

This article follows the card from Modern Horizons in 2019 through its principal archetypes, rules texture, sequencing, counterplay, material history, and the lesson it left for later deck builders.

The original card

II · The debut

The printed object before the reputation

The first verified paper printing appeared in Modern Horizons in 2019, with original artwork by Chase Stone. Its type line—Legendary Planeswalker — Wrenn—determined which tutors, removal spells, and rules systems could interact with it before players discovered its best shell.

The mana cost {R}{G} and mana value 2.0 define the visible price. Legacy repeatedly changes the practical price through fast mana, free spells, fetchlands, graveyards, and engines, but those systems do not erase the original rate; they explain why the rate became exploitable.

The design is best read as a package. A two-mana planeswalker converted fetchlands and Wasteland into repeatable resources while threatening to invalidate one-toughness creatures. That package created a role no single comparison captures, even when cards such as Wasteland or Misty Rainforest occupy neighboring territory.

III · The rules

The exact text creates the strategic ceiling

The current Oracle text reads: “[+1]: Return up to one target land card from your graveyard to your hand. [−1]: Wrenn and Six deals 1 damage to any target. [−7]: You get an emblem with "Instant and sorcery cards in your graveyard have retrace." (You may cast instant and sorcery cards from your graveyard by discarding a land card in addition to paying their other costs.)” Each cost, target, timing word, and restriction matters. The card is not merely shorthand for its most famous outcome; it is a sequence of legal actions that opponents can interrupt at specific points.

When using the card, announce choices and targets clearly, track alternate costs and zone changes, and separate cast triggers from resolution. Many of the card’s strongest historical interactions arose because Legacy decks could exploit those distinctions rather than because the printed effect was secretly larger than it appeared.

The first plus ability should usually recover the land that best fixes the next two turns, not merely the land with the highest abstract value. That practical rule is the bridge between knowing the text and converting it into an advantage.

IV · Legacy

Why the format made the card larger

Temur Delver and four-color shells used the card to turn mana denial into inevitability until its Legacy ban in 2019. Legacy supplied efficient mana, cantrips, tutors, and highly polarized matchups, so a card with a narrow-looking purpose could become a defining answer or engine.

The card’s most important homes were Temur Delver, Four-color control, Lands-adjacent midrange. In each shell, its function changed slightly: one deck used it to accelerate, another to protect a combo, another to create inevitability or punish an opposing assumption.

The shared thread was role compression. Drawing Wrenn and Six rarely meant drawing only one effect; it also changed how the opponent sequenced lands, held removal, sideboarded, or evaluated an opening hand.

V · Archetypes

The decks that gave the text a job

In Temur Delver, the card operated as the most direct expression of its text. The surrounding deck was built to make its cost, timing, or condition routine rather than exceptional.

Four-color control used the same object differently, often as a flexible bridge between proactive development and interaction. Cards such as Daze, Tarmogoyf, and Delver of Secrets show how the shell supplied the resources or pressure that the namesake needed.

Lands-adjacent midrange demonstrates the card’s portability. A card becomes historically prominent when more than one archetype can reinterpret it without erasing the reason it was powerful in the first place.

VI · Sequencing

The turn is where the text becomes history

The first plus ability should usually recover the land that best fixes the next two turns, not merely the land with the highest abstract value. The correct sequence often differs from the mana-efficient sequence because information, protection, and the opponent’s response window can be worth more than one unused mana.

Players should ask three questions before committing it: what answer does the opponent represent, which resource is hardest to replace, and whether the card is stronger now or after one more draw step. Legacy rewards holding a powerful card until it changes the entire exchange rather than merely generating immediate value.

Companions such as Life from the Loam and Oko, Thief of Crowns can conceal the plan, create redundancy, or force interaction first. The best lines use those cards to make the namesake the final question rather than the first exposed permanent.

VII · Construction

What the deck must provide

A successful list does not include Wrenn and Six because the card is famous. It includes enough enablers, mana, and complementary interaction to make the card’s best mode happen often while keeping its floor acceptable.

The archetype must also decide how many copies it can support. Four copies maximize access but magnify legendary, expensive, situational, or redundant draws. Fewer copies work when tutors, cantrips, or recursion can find the effect at the right time.

The most revealing companions are Wasteland, Misty Rainforest, Daze, Tarmogoyf, Delver of Secrets. They show whether the card belongs to a tempo package, a prison shell, a graveyard engine, a tutor toolbox, or a long-game control plan.

VIII · Mulligans

What an opening hand must already know

An opening hand containing Wrenn and Six is not automatically a keep. The hand must also contain the mana, enabler, or interaction that gives the card its historical role. A famous card stranded behind the wrong colors or missing support is still a mulligan liability.

Against Temur Delver, the card is often central enough that a hand with access to it can justify a slower development. Against faster combo or prison, the same hand may need immediate interaction from cards such as Wasteland or Misty Rainforest before the namesake matters.

Post-sideboard mulligans should account for the opponent’s hate. Keeping a hand that executes only one graveyard, artifact, creature, or land-based line invites a single sideboard card to end the game. The strongest keeps either protect the namesake or can win through a different resource system.

IX · Sideboarding

Preserve the role while changing the shell

Sideboarding with Wrenn and Six begins by deciding whether it remains an engine, becomes a tutor target, or should be trimmed because the opponent’s answers have improved. The card’s historical reputation should not prevent reducing copies when the matchup attacks its exact resource.

Bring in cards that force the opponent to divide answers. If the primary plan relies on legendary planeswalker — wrenn interaction, a secondary threat from another permanent type can make removal line up poorly. Likewise, protection is valuable only when it costs less tempo than simply presenting another threat.

The opponent will often bring broad hate rather than one answer to the card itself. Plan for graveyard exile, artifact destruction, sweepers, taxing permanents, or mana denial according to the archetype. A good sideboard plan preserves the strategic question while changing the object that asks it.

X · Matchups

Where the card is strongest and where it fails

The card is strongest when the opponent’s normal plan supplies the condition it punishes or cannot efficiently answer the permanent type. That is why its most memorable performances came in matchups where one side’s basic infrastructure—lands, spells, graveyard, creature density, or artifacts—made the namesake better automatically.

It is weakest when the opponent can ignore the axis entirely. A narrow hate card can be blank against fair pressure; a large engine can be too slow against combo; a creature-based answer can fail into sweepers. Knowing that floor is as important as remembering the ceiling.

Use the first game to identify which version of the opposing archetype is present. Legacy deck names hide meaningful differences in removal, basic lands, graveyard reliance, and speed. Wrenn and Six should be assigned a matchup role from observed cards, not from the label on a decklist.

XI · Counterplay

The answer is usually a window, not a card

Pressure the planeswalker immediately, protect utility creatures from the minus ability, and avoid keeping land-light hands that fold to recurring Wasteland. That is the practical starting point for playing against it.

Opponents should distinguish between answering the card and answering the engine around it. Removing one permanent may be irrelevant if the graveyard, hand, mana base, or tutor chain immediately produces another copy of the same function.

Sideboarding should therefore target the resource system as well as the visible card. The strongest answers either trade at favorable tempo, cover several related threats, or remain useful when the opponent presents a different plan.

XII · Metagame

What changed when both players knew it existed

Once Wrenn and Six became established, opponents adjusted before it was drawn. Mana bases changed, removal stayed in after sideboarding, graveyard hate appeared earlier, or players altered the order in which they exposed threats.

That invisible pressure is part of prominence. A card does not need to dominate every event to shape Legacy; it needs to make reasonable decks reserve slots, fetch different lands, or respect a line that would otherwise be safe.

A cheap planeswalker can be a mana engine, removal spell, and long-game emblem all at once; that compression can overwhelm a format built around efficient one-for-ones. This is the broader metagame lesson preserved by the card’s history.

XIII · Historical arc

From breakthrough to institution

Temur Delver and four-color shells used the card to turn mana denial into inevitability until its Legacy ban in 2019. Over time, new printings and new card pools changed the exact shell, but the strategic problem remained recognizable.

Sometimes the card became less common because a more efficient descendant appeared. Sometimes policy intervention, a rules change, or the loss of a companion ended an era. In other cases it settled into sideboards and specialist archetypes, where its influence became quieter but no less legible.

The archive treats that decline or transformation as part of the story rather than a verdict. Legacy cards often matter most at the moment they teach the format a new constraint.

XIV · Piloting

A field guide for the namesake card

First, identify whether the card is an engine, answer, lock piece, tutor target, or finisher in the current matchup. The same seventy-five can assign it a different job after sideboarding.

Second, preserve the resource the card converts. If it uses graveyard cards, creature count, artifacts, lands, life, or cards in hand, spending that resource casually lowers the ceiling before the namesake is drawn.

Third, plan the opponent’s cleanest answer. Pressure the planeswalker immediately, protect utility creatures from the minus ability, and avoid keeping land-light hands that fold to recurring Wasteland. The best pilots do not merely hope the answer is absent; they sequence so the answer arrives at the wrong time or trades for the wrong object.

XV · Design echoes

The idea after the original moment

Wasteland is a useful comparison because it reaches adjacent strategic territory through a different cost, card type, or timing restriction. The comparison should not be reduced to which card is “better.”

Design descendants preserve the question that mattered: a cheap planeswalker can be a mana engine, removal spell, and long-game emblem all at once; that compression can overwhelm a format built around efficient one-for-ones. Later cards may be safer, narrower, or more efficient, but the original makes the underlying exchange easier to see.

That continuity is one reason Wrenn and Six deserves an article outside the numbered thousand. Its history is not only a record of decks; it is a record of a design problem that Magic continued to revisit.

XVI · Artwork

The visual identity of the role

Chase Stone illustrated the original Modern Horizons printing. The artwork gave a specific face to a card that players would eventually remember as an engine, answer, or metagame warning rather than only as a fictional object.

Reprints changed borders, frames, finishes, and sometimes illustrations. Those material changes matter because tournament staples are handled, recognized, and remembered through repeated physical printings as much as through Oracle text.

XVII · Legacy

What the card taught the format

A cheap planeswalker can be a mana engine, removal spell, and long-game emblem all at once; that compression can overwhelm a format built around efficient one-for-ones.

The card’s lasting contribution is therefore larger than its current metagame share. It gave players a repeatable way to describe a matchup, an opening, a sideboard plan, or a deck-building cost.

That is the standard for this Honorable Mention expansion: not every card needed a permanent tier-one home, but each one changed how serious Legacy players built, sequenced, or prepared.
