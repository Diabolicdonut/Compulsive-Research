The Price of Every Color

City of Brass turned life into perfect mana, made five-color control practical, and remained useful from The Deck to Dredge because certainty was always worth paying for.

I · The Toll

The price of every color

City of Brass is one of Magic’s purest bargains. It asks no questions about the spell in your hand. White, blue, black, red, or green—the land will produce exactly what is required, immediately and without entering tapped. The price is paid in life, one point at a time.

That exchange sounds modest now, after decades of multicolor lands, fetchlands, shocklands, triomes, and Commander staples. In 1993 it was revolutionary. City of Brass did not merely help a two-color deck smooth its draws. It made the idea of a deck that could reach across the entire color pie feel practical.

Its history therefore runs through many different kinds of Magic. It supported the five-color control architecture of Brian Weissman’s The Deck, allowed Jakub Šlemr to disguise a rainbow mana base beneath a black aggressive shell, helped Brian Kibler’s Red Zone decks stretch into sideboard colors, and eventually became one of the canonical lands of Dredge. Whenever a strategy demanded exact colors more urgently than it valued its own life total, the city reopened its gates.

II · Rabiah

Arabian Nights opened the city

Arabian Nights was Magic’s first expansion and the first set to demonstrate that lands could carry personalities of their own. The base game had basic lands and the ten original dual lands. Arabian Nights introduced places that drew cards, animated creatures, stored power, damaged attackers, or regenerated Elephants. City of Brass belonged to this first great experiment in making a land more than a colored resource.

The card’s concept was both mechanical and evocative. A fabulous city offered access to everything—the whole spectrum of magical power—but life inside it was dangerous. The original wording made the location feel physically hostile: using or even disturbing it carried a cost.

The set’s other famous lands were narrow and spectacular. Library of Alexandria rewarded a full hand. Bazaar of Baghdad transformed cards in hand into graveyard velocity. Diamond Valley converted creatures into life. City of Brass was less visibly explosive, yet more universal. It did not create a new engine. It allowed every other engine to coexist.

Mark Tedin’s original Arabian Nights artworkThe composition gives City of Brass unusual scale through a muted palette, layered distance, and a strong silhouette. Rather than serving as a backdrop, the landscape feels ancient, watchful, and complete without any figure at its center.
III · The Constraint

Before perfect fixing

Early deck construction was defined by a blunt tension. Powerful cards existed in every color, but the mana base punished greed. The original dual lands solved two-color requirements elegantly, although they still forced a deck to choose particular pairs. A player who wanted Counterspell, Swords to Plowshares, Demonic Tutor, and Red Elemental Blast had to assemble several different duals and accept hands that produced the wrong half of the deck.

City of Brass changed the arithmetic because every copy counted as every color. The land did not replace duals; it connected them. A four-City mana base could support a primary blue-white shell while opening black tutoring, red sideboard cards, and green recursion without requiring equal numbers of each corresponding dual.

That flexibility was not merely convenience. It made singleton restricted cards more castable and made sideboards more ambitious. The card’s true text was therefore partly written on the deck-registration sheet: fifteen sideboard slots could now contain answers from colors the main deck barely played.

IV · The Promise

A land with no color preference

City of Brass has one of the cleanest outputs in the game: one mana of any color. There is no reveal condition, no charge counter, no requirement that another permanent be present, and no delay. The land is equally capable of casting a first-turn Duress, holding up Counterspell, or completing the fifth color for a late sideboard card.

Its strength comes from certainty. Many lands that appear painless are conditional. Reflecting Pool depends on the rest of the mana base. Forbidden Orchard grants the opponent a creature. Gemstone Mine eventually exhausts itself. City of Brass simply works until the game ends.

Speed

It enters untapped and can produce any color immediately.

Range

Every colored pip in the deck is accessible from the same land.

Reliability

No other permanent, land type, or counter is required.

Cost

Every tap threatens one damage, including taps caused by an opponent.

V · The Contract

The life point was the contract

The life loss is what kept the card from erasing color discipline entirely. A player could cast anything, but not forever. Four Cities in an opening hand represented perfect color access and a rapidly shrinking life total.

Importantly, City of Brass deals damage rather than causing loss of life. That distinction has changed in practical importance over the years, but it gives the card texture. Damage can be prevented, modified, or amplified. It also means that the city’s drawback feels like a source attacking its controller rather than an abstract payment.

The correct valuation depends on the deck. A control deck that plans to reach turn fifteen may tap the land only when no alternative exists. An aggressive deck may treat three or four damage as irrelevant if the colors allow its creatures to arrive on curve. A combo deck may regard nineteen life as functionally identical to twenty because the opponent is supposed to get only one more turn.

VI · The Machinery

The tap trigger matters

City of Brass does not say, “Whenever you tap City of Brass for mana.” It triggers whenever the land becomes tapped. That wording makes it more interactive—and more dangerous—than its later descendant Mana Confluence.

If the land attacks as a creature, becomes tapped by an artifact, or is targeted by an opposing effect that taps lands, the city still deals damage. Old cards such as Icy Manipulator could therefore turn mana denial into direct pressure. Later, Rishadan Port made the interaction famous in tournament play: tapping a City during upkeep could deny a color and inflict a point at the same time.

The trigger also creates a visible object on the stack. Producing the mana happens immediately because the mana ability does not use the stack, but the damage waits to resolve. That small gap produces the card’s most technical decisions.

VII · The Hostile Tap

When the opponent taps it

The most memorable downside is that the controller does not have exclusive authority over the pain. If an opponent taps City of Brass, its controller still takes the damage. This turns cards that normally tax mana into miniature burn spells.

Against Rishadan Port, a low-life player must decide whether to expose City of Brass during upkeep or use it on the preceding end step. Against Icy Manipulator, the land can become a liability even when no spell needs to be cast. Effects that freeze lands therefore attack both tempo and life.

The key wording
City of Brass cares that it became tapped, not who tapped it and not whether mana was produced.

This vulnerability is also why Mana Confluence is not a perfect aesthetic replacement. Mana Confluence asks for life only when its controller chooses to activate the colored-mana ability. City of Brass makes the city itself dangerous.

VIII · The Skyline

Mark Tedin’s impossible city

Mark Tedin’s Arabian Nights art is central to the card’s identity. The city does not resemble a practical settlement. It is a metallic dream packed into a frame: warm towers rise against a cool sky, and the architecture seems to continue beyond the visible edges.

The image communicates abundance without comfort. Every color may be available inside, but the city is dense, sharp, and inhospitable. The viewer is offered a panorama rather than a path. It is a place of access, not belonging.

Later illustrations interpreted the same idea in different ways—Tom Wänerstrand emphasized monumental fantasy architecture, Ron Walotsky produced a luminous urban expanse, and modern versions pushed the city toward grand digital spectacle. Yet Tedin’s original remains the image most closely tied to the card’s dangerous promise.

IX · The Foundation

The Deck needed every color

Brian Weissman’s The Deck is remembered as an early blueprint for control: trade resources efficiently, accumulate card advantage, stabilize the game, and win only when victory becomes inevitable. City of Brass was one of the cards that made the blueprint possible.

The core was blue-white, with Mana Drain, Counterspell, Swords to Plowshares, Disenchant, and Moat. But the deck also wanted black for Demonic Tutor, green for Regrowth, and red for Red Elemental Blast. Four Cities converted those splashes from aspiration into infrastructure.

The land’s life cost aligned with control theory. Life was a resource, and unused life at the end of a won game had no value. If one point bought the exact answer required to protect a long-term advantage, the transaction was favorable.

X · The Reprint

Chronicles made the city public

The Arabian Nights printing was scarce and increasingly expensive. Chronicles brought City of Brass into a much wider card pool in 1995. Unlike some cards whose reprints merely preserved nostalgia, City of Brass was a four-copy piece of mana infrastructure. Increased availability directly changed what ordinary players could build.

The white-bordered Chronicles card also became one of the emblematic objects in the controversy surrounding early reprints. Collectors saw supply expand abruptly; players saw a foundational multicolor land become attainable. Both reactions helped shape the policies and expectations that later governed Magic’s relationship with its earliest cards.

Because City of Brass was not placed on the Reserved List, its history continued through core sets, Masters products, promotional releases, and specialty treatments. The city became a recurring piece of Magic rather than a sealed relic of 1993.

XI · The Institution

The long core-set residency

Fifth Edition introduced Tom Wänerstrand’s new illustration and placed City of Brass into a period when core sets defined the baseline vocabulary of tournament Magic. Sixth Edition retained the card; Seventh Edition introduced Ron Walotsky’s art and a black-bordered foil; Eighth Edition carried the land into the modern card frame.

This sequence matters because it kept City of Brass legal through radically different Standard environments. The card did not belong to one mechanic or block theme. It was generic enough to support almost any multicolor strategy and iconic enough to return whenever R&D was willing to permit universal fixing.

Its presence also meant that the first Modern card pool inherited City of Brass. Eighth Edition was part of Modern’s original legality boundary, so a land born in Arabian Nights entered the new nonrotating format through a core-set reprint.

XII · The Rainbow

Worlds 1997 went prismatic

The 1997 World Championship demonstrated how thoroughly players had learned to treat City of Brass as strategic infrastructure. The defining black aggressive deck was not truly mono-black. The blue-red tempo deck was not satisfied with two colors. Sideboards reached across the color pie because the mana bases could support them.

City of Brass worked beside Gemstone Mine and Undiscovered Paradise, creating a generation of “rainbow” lands that let decks preserve a dominant base color while borrowing the most efficient effects elsewhere.

The result was not undisciplined five-color good-stuff. These decks remained coherent. City of Brass allowed them to answer narrow tournament questions without rebuilding their central engines.

XIII · The Inheritance

Modern inherited an ancient land

Because City of Brass appeared in Eighth Edition, it entered Modern when the format was created. Few cards from Magic’s first expansion can make that claim through an ordinary tournament-legal reprint.

Modern eventually received powerful competitors: fetchland-shockland mana bases, fast lands, tribal lands, and Mana Confluence. City of Brass therefore became specialized rather than universal. It remained most attractive where all five colors mattered immediately or where the deck did not want to pay life up front for a shockland that might still fail to produce every color.

The card’s Modern career is a form of historical continuity. A solution invented before most tournament formats existed remained technically and strategically relevant in a format launched nearly two decades later.

XIV · The Hundred Cards

Commander made one-of fixing matter

Commander gave City of Brass a new kind of relevance. Singleton construction increases the value of redundant five-color lands, and a forty-life starting total makes individual points of damage easier to absorb.

In a five-color Commander deck, City of Brass is rarely the entire mana plan. It is one reliable node among Command Tower, Mana Confluence, Exotic Orchard, fetchlands, and typed duals. Its greatest strength is that it has no setup requirement and no color-identity restriction beyond the deck’s own commander.

The card can still be punished in multiplayer. Repeated tap effects or copies of Manabarbs can magnify the damage, and a game that lasts ten turns may make the city more expensive than it first appeared. Yet the format’s scale generally favors access over thrift.

XV · The Lineage

The descendants of the bargain

City of Brass established a durable design pattern: perfect color fixing must carry a meaningful cost. Later lands changed the form of the payment while preserving the central promise.

Visions · 1997

Undiscovered Paradise

Undiscovered Paradise returned to hand.

Undiscovered Paradise returned to hand. Tarnished Citadel offered painless colorless mana but demanded three life for color. Ancient Ziggurat restricted what the mana could cast. Each descendant asks the same design question City of Brass first made famous: what is perfect color worth?

XVII · The Technique

How to use City of Brass

Spend basics first when the color is available

City of Brass is most valuable as an option. If a basic or dual land can produce the required color without damage, use it and preserve the City for a later turn when only perfect fixing will work.

Plan around opposing tap effects

Against Rishadan Port or Icy Manipulator, decide whether the land should be tapped proactively. The opponent may otherwise choose both the timing of the mana denial and the damage.

Count the entire line, not one activation

A hand that appears to cost one life may actually require the City on three consecutive turns. Aggressive and combo decks can accept that; control decks may need to sequence a different land first.

Respect one-life rules

The damage trigger normally resolves before the spell cast with the mana. At one life, identify whether an instant-speed response can resolve before the trigger rather than assuming the spell will save you.

Use the sideboard freedom

City of Brass is at its best when the deck exploits all of its text. A mana base that includes the land can often support narrow off-color sideboard cards without compromising the main plan.

XVIII · The Feeling

What players remember

Players remember City of Brass as the land that made impossible hands look keepable. A Swamp, a City, and a collection of mismatched spells could still become a coherent opening because the city promised the missing color.

They also remember the arithmetic. Every tap was announced, every point recorded, and every Port activation carried a small cruelty. The land made the mana base visible as part of play rather than a silent foundation beneath it.

City of Brass did not ask what color your deck was. It asked how much life you were willing to spend proving that the question did not matter.
Compulsive Research · reconstructed player memory

That combination—freedom followed by consequence—is why the card remains iconic. It is not simply a five-color land. It is one of Magic’s clearest statements that power is most interesting when it comes with a bill.
