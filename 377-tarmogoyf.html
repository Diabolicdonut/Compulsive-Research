I · Opening hand

The Creature That Turned Every Graveyard into a Power Meter

The card

Tarmogoyf

{1}{G}
Creature — Lhurgoyf

Tarmogoyf’s power is equal to the number of card types among cards in all graveyards and its toughness is equal to that number plus 1.

First releaseFuture Sight · 2007
Archive identityEntry 377 · Modern Foundations

Tarmogoyf is a creature whose text reads like bookkeeping. Its power equals the number of card types among all cards in all graveyards, and its toughness is one higher. There is no enters-the-battlefield trigger, no keyword, and no protection. The card simply asks what kind of game has already happened.

That question proved revolutionary. Fetch lands supplied land, cantrips and discard supplied sorcery, removal and countermagic supplied instant, and the first dead creature supplied creature. In ordinary constructed play, Tarmogoyf often became a 3/4 or 4/5 without its controller devoting a single card to growing it.

The efficient body crossed archetype borders almost immediately. It attacked in Zoo and Jund, defended planeswalkers in blue control, anchored Canadian Threshold, and gave combo-control decks a cheap fair plan. For years, every two-mana creature was measured against the same question: does it pass the Tarmogoyf test?

Its later decline is equally instructive. Tarmogoyf did not become weaker under the rules. Creatures around it began generating cards, mana, or immediate effects, while graveyard hate became more common. The benchmark remained excellent at size, but size alone stopped being the entire exam.

II · Origin

Future Sight printed a joke-looking rules puzzle

Future Sight deliberately presented cards as artifacts from possible futures. Tarmogoyf’s reminder text famously listed card types and included tribal, a type that barely existed when the set arrived. The card therefore functioned both as a tournament creature and as a preview of rules vocabulary.

Early evaluations often focused on how hard it seemed to make the creature large. The breakthrough was recognizing that constructed decks already place multiple card types in graveyards through normal play. Tarmogoyf did not need a dedicated engine; it needed fetch lands, cheap spells, and combat.

Justin Murray’s original art makes the creature feel less like an individual monster than a biological response to accumulated waste. The graveyard is not merely where the Goyf lives—it is the environmental record that determines its body.

Justin Murray’s original Future Sight artworkA hulking, many-limbed predator crowds the frame, its gaping maw and moss-dark hide dissolving into a sickly green haze. The close, almost airless composition makes the creature feel less observed than suddenly encountered.
III · Rules

Both graveyards, eight card types, one characteristic ability

Tarmogoyf counts card types, not supertypes or subtypes. Artifact, battle, creature, enchantment, instant, kindred, land, planeswalker, and sorcery are the relevant modern types; “legendary,” “basic,” “Goblin,” and similar labels do not add to the total.

It counts both graveyards. An opponent’s fetch land and instant can grow your creature even if your own graveyard is empty. This is why the card remains functional in decks that do not deliberately self-mill.

The ability defines power and toughness in every zone. A Tarmogoyf in the graveyard has the same characteristic calculation, which matters for effects that check creature power outside the battlefield.

Toughness is always one greater than power. That extra point is strategically central: a 3/4 survives Lightning Bolt, and a 4/5 attacks through many creatures while resisting common damage-based removal.

IV · Growth economy

The first three types are cheap; the fifth is a decision

Land, instant, and sorcery are easy to establish with fetch lands and normal spells. Creature usually follows through combat or removal. Reaching enchantment, artifact, planeswalker, battle, or kindred often requires either the opponent’s deck or a deliberate inclusion.

Good deck building does not weaken every slot merely to add types. The classic lesson is selective support: cards such as Mishra’s Bauble, Seal of Fire, or planeswalkers can perform real jobs while improving the ceiling.

Because all copies share the same graveyard count, one enabling card grows every Tarmogoyf. That scaling made small deck-building concessions more attractive than they would be for a creature with individual counters.

V · Tournament history

The strange rare became the default threat

By Pro Tour Valencia in 2007, Remi Fortier’s Next Level Blue showed that Tarmogoyf was not only an aggro card. A blue control deck could splash green for a two-mana finisher while using Counterbalance, Sensei’s Divining Top, and Vedalken Shackles to control the game.

Uri Peleg’s 2007 World Championship Rock deck and Charles Gindy’s Pro Tour Hollywood victory placed Tarmogoyf inside black-green attrition. The creature fit perfectly beside discard and removal because those spells both protected it and increased its size.

Brian Kibler’s Pro Tour Austin Zoo deck used Tarmogoyf as the middle of an aggressive curve. In Legacy, Canadian Threshold and later RUG Delver treated it as a threat that could be deployed cheaply while leaving mana for Daze, Stifle, and protection.

Modern kept the card central through Jund, Abzan, Death’s Shadow, and graveyard-oriented midrange. David Mines’s 2019 Mythic Championship finish demonstrated that even after years of creature escalation, a one-mana 5/5 Hollow One deck could still want Tarmogoyf as its reliable two-drop.

VI · Construction

Splashable power made green a single-card color

Tarmogoyf costs only one green mana. That detail allowed blue, black, and red decks to splash it without becoming fundamentally green. Fetch lands and shock lands made the splash trivial in Extended and Modern; dual lands did the same in Legacy.

The card rewards spell diversity but punishes graveyard replacement effects. Decks using Rest in Peace, Leyline of the Void, or heavy delve must understand that their own plan can shrink the creature.

Four copies were normal when Tarmogoyf was the primary threat. Lower counts make sense when the deck wants only a robust secondary plan or when drawing multiples into graveyard hate is risky.

VII · Sequencing

Cast before combat, count after resolution

Always recount card types after a spell resolves. A sorcery that destroys an artifact can add sorcery and artifact simultaneously, changing combat by two points before blockers.

Fetch lands create instant-speed growth. Sacrificing a fetch after blocks can add land to a previously landless graveyard and save Tarmogoyf in combat. Opponents must account for every uncracked fetch.

When holding a planeswalker or enchantment, decide whether the extra type matters immediately. Casting the card precombat may expose it to countermagic but can turn a trade into a clean attack.

Delve spells and escape costs remove cards, not types in the abstract. Exile redundant cards while preserving at least one card of each valuable type whenever the size matters.

VIII · Counterplay

Shrink the graveyard, ignore the body, or answer it cleanly

Exile-based removal is ideal because it answers the creature without adding another creature card to a graveyard. Bounce and temporary removal can also reset tempo, but the Goyf itself remains cheap to replay.

Graveyard hate may shrink Tarmogoyf dramatically. Rest in Peace makes it a 0/1 because cards never remain in graveyards. One-shot exile can function as a combat trick if it removes the only card of one or more types.

Do not overinvest in damage removal without recounting. Lightning Bolt may fail against a 3/4 but succeed after a graveyard is exiled. Conversely, casting the Bolt first may add instant and increase toughness before damage resolves.

Modern creatures with immediate value changed the strategic answer: sometimes the right response is not to kill Tarmogoyf but to go wider, fly over it, or generate enough cards that a large vanilla body cannot keep pace.

IX · Format migration

The benchmark remained while the exam changed

Legacy once treated Tarmogoyf as the premier threat for tempo and midrange. It remains legal, but newer threats often provide card selection, graveyard value, or immediate pressure while also surviving common removal.

Modern still supports the card in Jund-style decks, Death’s Shadow shells, and strategies that fill card types quickly. Its stock rises when efficient removal and fair creature combat dominate, and falls when combo, exile effects, or value creatures define the environment.

Commander is a poor natural home because one large attacker without evasion or multiplayer scaling does little against three opponents. The card’s reputation is rooted in sixty-card efficiency.

Pioneer excludes Future Sight by chronology, preserving Tarmogoyf as a symbol of Modern and eternal formats rather than a universal green staple.

X · Art and memory

The price tag became part of the card text

For years Tarmogoyf’s secondary-market price made it a cultural object as much as a deck card. Reprints in Modern Masters products were major events, and opening one carried the feeling of finding the format’s defining rare.

The card also generated a vocabulary. “Goyf math” meant counting graveyard types; “the Tarmogoyf test” asked whether removal, blockers, and other creatures could compete with a two-mana body of that size.

Its lack of spectacle strengthened the myth. Tarmogoyf won by being present earlier and larger than expected, not by producing an elaborate trigger. The opponent simply discovered that ordinary Magic had built the monster for free.

XI · Design echo

The Goyf returned with a personal graveyard

Nethergoyf is an explicit descendant that preserves the variable body while changing the accounting system.

A comparative echo

Nethergoyf

It counts card types only in its controller’s graveyard, costs one mana, and can escape, trading the original’s shared-environment scaling for recursion and a narrower resource base.

The comparison shows how modern designs add resilience or card advantage to a rate creature while placing clearer limits on effortless growth.

XII · Decision point

The graveyard is a shared combat zone

A 3/4 Tarmogoyf attacks into a 4/4 blocker. The graveyards contain land, instant, and creature. The attacker’s controller holds a sorcery; the defender holds an instant-speed artifact removal spell targeting their own expendable artifact.

Casting the sorcery before damage makes Tarmogoyf 4/5. The defender can respond by destroying the artifact, adding artifact and making it 5/6—helping the attacker rather than the blocker. But if the artifact removal exiles the graveyard card instead, the calculation changes in the opposite direction.

The example illustrates why Tarmogoyf combat is not local to the battlefield. Every spell, sacrifice, and exile can change the creature before damage.

XIII · Legacy

A fair card that changed what fair meant

Tarmogoyf did not create mana, draw cards, or lock the opponent out. It changed competitive Magic by making raw creature rate efficient enough that control and tempo decks could end games without dedicating many slots to finishers.

Later creatures surpassed it by combining acceptable stats with immediate value. That does not erase the design achievement; it shows how thoroughly the card reset expectations.

Whenever a new two-drop is praised for being “more than a Tarmogoyf,” the original is still doing its historical work as the measuring stick.
