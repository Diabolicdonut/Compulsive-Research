Entry 057 · The Land That Made Discard Valuable

Everything Must Go

Bazaar of Baghdad began as a land that appeared to lose cards and mana. Then reanimation, Squee, dredge, and recursive creatures turned its bad bargain into one of Vintage’s defining engines.

I · The Exchange

Everything must go

Some Magic cards look powerful before anyone shuffles them. Bazaar of Baghdad looked like the opposite. It occupied a land drop without producing mana, drew two cards, and then demanded three cards in return. In the language players would later use, the transaction was minus one card before the lost mana source was even counted.

That apparent defect became the card’s identity. Bazaar does not reward a deck for accepting card disadvantage. It rewards a deck for refusing to recognize the graveyard as a place where cards have been lost. Once discarded cards could return, be cast, be dredged, or trigger other objects, the old arithmetic collapsed. The Bazaar had not been charging too much. Players had been valuing the wrong side of the exchange.

The original object

Bazaar of Baghdad

Land

{T}: Draw two cards, then discard three cards.

First printingArabian Nights · 1993
Original artistJeff A. Menges
Original rarityUncommon (U3)
Historical roleGraveyard engine and format pillar

Today, the card is among the most recognizable lands in Vintage. Entire decks begin with the question “Do I have Bazaar?” and build their mulligan rules, sideboards, and even their mana bases around the answer. Few cards have traveled farther from first impression to final reputation.

II · The First Expansion

Arabian Nights made lands do impossible things

Arabian Nights was Magic’s first expansion and its first sustained experiment with lands that behaved like spells. Library of Alexandria drew cards. Diamond Valley sacrificed creatures for life. Elephant Graveyard regenerated Elephants. Bazaar of Baghdad filtered a hand without asking for mana.

These lands were designed before the long-term value of a spell stapled to a land slot was fully understood. A player could not counter the playing of Bazaar with an ordinary counterspell, and the card did not consume a spell slot in deck construction. Its balancing mechanisms were severe: no mana production, immediate card loss, and vulnerability to land destruction. Those costs were real, but they were calibrated for a game in which the graveyard was mostly a record of what had already happened.

Arabian Nights turned geography into mechanics. The Library represented accumulated knowledge; the Bazaar represented exchange. The design became dangerous when Magic learned that a market could trade in things more valuable than cards in hand.

III · The Bad Bargain

The printed arithmetic is supposed to look wrong

Activate Bazaar with five other cards in hand. Draw two, rising to seven. Discard three, falling to four. The activation has reduced the hand by one card, and the land that performed it could not produce mana that turn. Compared with a normal draw spell, the exchange appears indefensible.

But raw card count is only one measure of value. Bazaar sees two new cards, chooses three cards from the expanded hand, and moves those cards to a zone that many decks can exploit. In a reanimator deck, discarding a giant creature may be equivalent to tutoring it into position. With Squee, Goblin Nabob, the discarded card returns. With dredge, the “draws” become large self-mill events. With madness, the discard becomes permission to cast. With Vengevine or Ichorid, the graveyard becomes a staging area for threats.

Bazaar of Baghdad did not become powerful because its drawback disappeared. It became powerful because decks learned to invoice the graveyard.The market’s real currency

The card is therefore one of Magic’s best lessons in contextual evaluation. A line of rules text cannot be priced in isolation from the zones, replacement effects, and recursive cards surrounding it.

IV · The Cost

A land that refuses to be a land

Bazaar of Baghdad has no mana ability. That fact is not decorative. A deck that plays four copies is effectively beginning with four lands that cannot cast a spell, pay for a tax, or help recover from a slow opening. Traditional decks cannot absorb that cost merely because card selection is attractive.

This separates Bazaar from most famous utility lands. Mishra’s Factory can attack and still make mana. Wasteland can disrupt and still produce colorless mana. Library of Alexandria has a spectacular ceiling and a functional floor. Bazaar’s floor is a blank land drop.

The decks that succeed with Bazaar either need very little mana, generate resources from the graveyard, or compensate with artifact acceleration and alternative costs. Vintage Dredge can play turns in which the only permanent it needs is Bazaar. Survival decks use Moxen, Spirit Guides, and actual colored lands to carry the mana burden. The cost remains visible in every successful shell.

V · The Procedure

Draw two, then discard three—without a pause

Bazaar’s ability resolves as one instruction. The controller draws two cards and then discards three before receiving priority. A player cannot draw the first card, cast it, draw the second, and then decide what to discard. The entire market transaction completes before anyone may act.

Dredge complicates the appearance but not the structure. Each draw can be replaced separately by a dredge ability. The controller might dredge twice, draw once and dredge once, or draw twice. After those replacements are completed, the controller discards three cards from whatever hand remains.

Because the ability does not produce mana, it is not a mana ability. Pithing Needle can stop it, Stifle can counter the activation, and effects that prevent activated abilities can matter. Destroying Bazaar after it has been activated does not remove the ability from the stack. The stalls may be demolished, but the current trade still settles.

VI · The First Great Partner

Squee changed the exchange rate

Squee, Goblin Nabob supplied the first beautifully repeatable answer to Bazaar’s card loss. If Squee is discarded, he returns to his owner’s hand at the beginning of the next upkeep. Two Squees can repeatedly absorb two of the three required discards, turning Bazaar into a powerful selection engine whose nominal losses replenish themselves.

This interaction mattered in several families of decks. Reanimator and Worldgorger Dragon lists could use Squee as discard fodder while digging for combo pieces. Survival of the Fittest could repeatedly discard Squee to search for creatures, then recover him for the next activation. Later Bazaar control and CounterVine strategies used Squee or Master of Death to preserve hand size while filtering every turn.

Squee did not make Bazaar generically good. The land still failed to produce mana and still required a deck willing to devote slots to recursive material. What Squee proved was more important: the three-card payment could be engineered rather than endured.

VII · The Loading Dock

Reanimator knew where discarded monsters belonged

Reanimation was an intuitive early home. Bazaar could find Animate Dead while depositing Nicol Bolas, Triskelion, or another enormous creature in the graveyard. The supposed card disadvantage became setup: the reanimation spell was in hand, the target was where the spell required it, and irrelevant cards had been exchanged for fresh looks.

Old School reanimator remains one of the clearest demonstrations of the card’s original-era potential. The decks were less deterministic than later graveyard engines, and they paid a genuine cost for non-mana lands, but Bazaar supplied something early Magic rarely offered: repeatable, uncounterable hand sculpting from the battlefield.

The same concept would echo through every later use. Bazaar is strongest when “discard” is not cleanup but delivery.

VIII · The Loop

Worldgorger Dragon turned the Bazaar into a win condition

Early-2000s Vintage found a more intricate use in Worldgorger Dragon combo. Enchanting the Dragon with Animate Dead–style effects could create a loop in which permanents repeatedly left and returned to the battlefield, untapping lands and generating mana. Bazaar helped assemble the graveyard and enchantment pieces, but it could also become part of the loop’s machinery.

Repeated Bazaar activations let the pilot move through the library while Squee replenished discard material. Once the deck found an outlet such as Ambassador Laquatus or a draw spell that could target the opponent, the mana loop became lethal. The card that appeared to lose resources had become a searchable, repeatable route through the entire deck.

Dragon was also an early warning about graveyard combo’s fragility. A mistimed removal spell could interrupt the loop catastrophically, and graveyard hate attacked both setup and execution. Bazaar made the deck consistent, but it could not make the graveyard safe.

IX · The Rewrite

Dredge replaced drawing with excavation

The dredge mechanic, introduced in Ravnica: City of Guilds, transformed Bazaar more completely than any prior card. A card with dredge in the graveyard may replace a draw: mill a specified number of cards, then return that dredger to hand. Bazaar offers two draws in one activation, and each can be replaced.

Activate Bazaar with Golgari Grave-Troll and Stinkweed Imp in the graveyard. Replace the first draw by milling six and returning the Troll. Replace the second by milling five and returning the Imp. Then discard three cards—including the dredgers just returned—so they are ready again for the next draw step or Bazaar activation.

The activation that once read “lose a card” now reads more like “mill eleven, select three cards to place in the graveyard, and reset the dredge engine.” The rules text did not change. The game around it did.

X · The Engine

A Bazaar turn is a chain of replacement effects

Vintage Dredge often begins before it casts anything. The opening Bazaar is played and activated. Dredgers enter or return from the graveyard. Milled Narcomoeba cards enter the battlefield. Bridge from Below waits in the graveyard. Ichorid, Prized Amalgam, and Silversmote Ghoul prepare future waves. Creeping Chill converts milling into direct life swings.

The deck’s “hand” rapidly becomes less important than the composition of its graveyard. Cabal Therapy can be cast by sacrificing a creature, stripping the opponent’s answer while Bridge creates Zombies. Dread Return can reanimate a finisher without mana. Ichorid returns during upkeep. Bazaar continues to dredge on demand.

This is why the archetype feels like a different game. Normal Magic moves resources from library to hand to stack to battlefield and eventually graveyard. Bazaar Dredge short-circuits that route. It uses the library as fuel, the graveyard as hand, and free triggers as mana.

XI · The Opening Hand

The first game is finding the land

Most decks mulligan to a functional mixture of lands and spells. Bazaar decks frequently mulligan toward one named card. A Dredge hand without Bazaar may contain powerful-looking pieces but lack a way to begin. A small hand with Bazaar, a dredger, and minimal support may be far stronger.

This creates one of Vintage’s most distinctive mulligan disciplines. The pilot must evaluate not only whether Bazaar is present, but whether it survives likely interaction, whether the hand can beat graveyard hate, and whether a backup plan exists. Petrified Field can recover a destroyed Bazaar. Free disruption can protect the first activation. Post-sideboard hands may need an answer to Leyline of the Void before the engine matters.

The correct keep is therefore both brutally narrow and highly contextual. “Bazaar or mulligan” is a useful slogan, not a complete decision tree.

XII · The Search

Serum Powder made mulligans part of deck construction

Serum Powder is one of the strangest cards ever to become a four-of staple, and Bazaar explains why. If Serum Powder is in an opening hand that a player could mulligan, its special action can exile that entire hand and draw the same number of cards. The process may be repeated with additional Powders.

Dredge is willing to accept the exile because the value of starting with Bazaar is so high. The deck is built with redundancy among dredgers, recursive creatures, and free disruption, making many exiled cards replaceable. The Powder itself may never be cast; its job is to alter the probability distribution of opening hands.

That construction choice reveals Bazaar’s gravitational force. It does not merely determine what happens after turn one. It changes which sixty cards are registered and how the pregame is played.

XIII · The Inversion

A Vintage deck learned to play without mana

Bazaar enabled versions of Dredge that could function with almost no conventional mana. Creatures returned from the graveyard. Therapies and Dread Returns used flashback costs paid with creatures. Unmask, Force of Will, Force of Negation, Grief, and other pitch spells interacted without lands. Hollow One could cost zero after discards.

The result was not literally a deck without costs. It paid in cards, graveyard position, creature sacrifices, and deck-building constraints. But it escaped the battlefield economy that cards such as Mishra’s Workshop and Sphere of Resistance were designed to attack. A taxing effect that made spells cost more could be nearly irrelevant when the Bazaar deck did not intend to cast those spells normally.

This strategic independence helped establish Dredge as a Vintage pillar. Opponents could not rely solely on efficient counters and mana denial; they needed targeted graveyard interaction.

XIV · The Free Board

Narcomoeba and Bridge made milling create permanents

Dredge became truly explosive when milling itself began to create a battlefield. Narcomoeba enters when milled from the library. Bridge from Below creates Zombies when the Dredge player’s nontoken creatures die while Bridge is in the graveyard. Dread Return converts three creatures into a reanimated threat.

Bazaar is the ignition switch because it mills through dredge while also placing drawn engine pieces back into the graveyard. One activation can reveal multiple Narcomoebas, Bridges, Therapies, and recursive creatures. The deck can move from an empty battlefield to a developed board without resolving a conventional creature spell.

Future designs repeatedly had to account for this infrastructure. A graveyard card did not merely interact with Dredge in the abstract; it interacted with four unrestricted copies of Bazaar of Baghdad.

XV · The Arms Race

Every sideboard learned the name of the graveyard

Vintage sideboards have long devoted substantial space to Bazaar strategies. Leyline of the Void can begin on the battlefield and shut off dredge before Bazaar activates. Ravenous Trap punishes a large graveyard turn for no mana. Tormod’s Crypt, Grafdigger’s Cage, Rest in Peace, and specialized effects attack different pieces of the engine.

Dredge responded by evolving. Chain of Vapor and Nature’s Claim answered permanents. Ingot Chewer attacked artifacts. Force of Vigor offered a free answer that could be used even in a deck with almost no mana. Some lists transformed toward Hollow One and creature pressure, forcing opponents to respect both the graveyard and the battlefield.

The result is a recurring Vintage ritual: game one demonstrates the engine’s raw power; post-sideboard games become a contest over hate, anti-hate, mulligans, and timing. Bazaar is the reason entire matches can change genre after sideboarding.

XVI · The Pillar

Vintage permits the full playset

Bazaar of Baghdad is legal as a four-of in Vintage. That makes it unusual among the format’s most famous early engines. Library of Alexandria is restricted; so are many tutors, draw spells, and accelerants. Bazaar instead supports an entire macro-archetype built around seeing it consistently.

The practical effect is structural. Vintage is often described through pillars: blue decks, Mishra’s Workshop decks, Bazaar decks, and the hybrid strategies between them. Bazaar is not simply included in Dredge. It is the axis around which Dredge, Survival, HollowVine, Squee control, and other graveyard shells can be organized.

This does not mean every Vintage deck fears the same Bazaar list. The card’s history is a sequence of shells, each converting discard into a different form of advantage.

XVII · The Closed Gate

Legacy cannot absorb the same transaction

Legacy bans Bazaar of Baghdad. The format has abundant graveyard tools—reanimation, dredge, madness, recursive creatures, and free interaction—but lacks Vintage’s restricted-card framework. Allowing four Bazaars would introduce the same opening consistency without requiring the rest of Vintage’s power structure.

The ban also shows the difference between a card being narrow and a card being safe. Bazaar belongs only in decks built to exploit it, but in those decks its effect is nearly irreplaceable. A narrow card can still be format-defining when the narrow strategy is powerful enough.

Legacy versions of Dredge have historically used Careful Study, Faithless Looting, Cephalid Coliseum, and similar effects. Those cards cost mana, are spells that can be countered, or require setup. Bazaar’s land-based repetition is the missing level.

XVIII · The Creature Market

Survival, Vengevine, and Hollow One opened another wing

Bazaar does not require dredge to be broken. Creature engines found a different conversion. Discard Basking Rootwalla and cast it through madness. Discard Vengevine, then cast two creatures to return it. Discard enough cards and Hollow One becomes free. Use Squee to refill the hand and Survival of the Fittest to turn every creature into the next required piece.

The interaction can create startling first turns: Bazaar draws two and discards three; Rootwalla enters through madness; Hollow One is cast for zero; Vengevine sees the second creature spell and returns with haste. A land activation has produced selection, discard synergies, and a battlefield.

These Survival and HollowVine decks broadened Bazaar’s identity. It was no longer only the land of a graveyard combo deck. It could support aggressive creature strategies, disruptive toolboxes, and long games powered by recursive cards.

XIX · The Long Market

Bazaar can be a control engine when the inventory returns

Squee-based Bazaar control turns the card’s famous drawback into a slow source of selection. Recurring cards such as Squee and Master of Death return to hand, absorb discards, and allow the controller to keep the best new cards. Free counterspells protect the engine while Hollow One or Vengevine provides pressure.

This branch plays very differently from Dredge. It may care about cards in hand, make ordinary land drops, and interact on the stack. Bazaar is not used to dump the entire library into the graveyard as quickly as possible. It is used to sculpt a hand repeatedly while turning recursive cards into a renewable currency.

The distinction matters because opposing hate must be chosen carefully. A card excellent against Bridge from Below may be mediocre against Hollow One. Bazaar is one card, but it supports multiple economies.

XX · The Singleton Question

Commander asks whether one Bazaar is worth a land drop

Bazaar of Baghdad is legal in Commander, but singleton construction changes the calculus. A deck cannot reliably build every opening around one copy, and a non-mana land is costly in a format where commanders and engines often require substantial colored mana.

It can still be exceptional in deliberate homes: reanimator, madness, dredge, Squee recursion, lands decks that can tutor utility lands, and commanders that reward discarding. The activation is repeatable, costs no mana, and can place several chosen cards into the graveyard at once.

Yet fame is not sufficient reason to include it. In a deck that merely “uses the graveyard sometimes,” Bazaar may produce awkward hands and real card loss. The card demands a thesis. Its power comes from making the discard step productive, not from being an expensive old land.

XXI · The Image

Jeff A. Menges painted motion, density, and exchange

Jeff A. Menges’s original illustration presents the Bazaar as a crowded field of tents, figures, goods, and movement. Unlike the isolated monument of Library of Alexandria, the scene has no single visual center. The eye moves through it as a player’s hand moves through the activation—new objects appear, old ones leave, and value is found in the exchange.

The image also belongs unmistakably to Arabian Nights, a set that mixed legendary stories with real place names and a storybook vision of markets, deserts, cities, and palaces. The card’s later tournament identity is mechanical and abstract, but its visual identity remains a busy public place.

The alternate image is important because the Reserved List prevented an ordinary paper reprint; digital releases became the place where Bazaar could receive new presentation without creating new tournament-legal paper supply.

XXII · The Imitations

The descendants kept the text and added a bill

Later designs repeatedly returned to Bazaar’s shape while adding costs that make the effect answerable. Magus of the Bazaar places the ability on a fragile creature that must survive summoning sickness. Bazaar Trademage provides a single Bazaar-like exchange when it enters the battlefield, attached to a creature and a mana cost.

These cards reveal how difficult Bazaar is to reproduce fairly. Add mana, summoning sickness, a one-shot trigger, a sacrifice, or a threshold condition, and the effect becomes a tool rather than an ecosystem. Bazaar itself asks only for a land drop and a tap.

XXIII · The Object

The Reserved List made the engine a collectible barrier

The only normal tournament-legal paper printing of Bazaar of Baghdad is the Arabian Nights card. Masters Edition III and Vintage Masters are digital releases; the Vintage Championship item is oversized memorabilia rather than a playable reprint. Reserved List policy prevents a conventional paper reissue.

That scarcity has practical consequences. Vintage events often permit proxies because a deck may require four Bazaars, not merely one restricted collectible. Commander players considering a single copy confront a price created by tiny original supply, collector demand, and specialized competitive demand.

Bazaar is therefore both a gameplay pillar and an access problem. Its history cannot be separated from the physical reality that the archetype’s central four-of exists in paper only as a 1993 uncommon.

XXIV · The Technique

How to operate the market

Know what the activation is buying

Do not activate Bazaar merely because it is available. In Dredge, identify which draws will be replaced and which cards need to return to the graveyard. In Survival, plan madness and Vengevine triggers. In Squee control, preserve enough renewable discard material to prevent the hand from collapsing.

Sequence around interaction

Against Wasteland, activating immediately may secure value. Against Stifle, the timing may expose the engine. Post-sideboard, determine whether graveyard hate must be removed before the first activation or whether a Hollow One plan can pressure through it.

Mulligan for a plan, not a logo

A Bazaar hand without a functional engine can still fail. A hand with Bazaar, dredgers, and protection is different from a hand with Bazaar and six cards that do nothing from the graveyard. Serum Powder decisions must also account for unique sideboard cards and cards that cannot safely be exiled.

Count the discard

The ability always asks for three cards if available. Plan what will be in hand after draw replacements. A player who dredges twice may return two dredgers, but still needs a third discard. Careless sequencing can force the loss of an answer or anti-hate card.

Protect the land when the game requires it

Petrified Field, additional Bazaars, and alternative discard outlets can matter. Some hands can survive one Wasteland; others are entirely dependent on the first activation. Know which game you are playing before committing resources.

XXV · The Feeling

What players remember

Play a land. Tap it. Replace two draws. Put a dozen cards in the graveyard. Suddenly the empty battlefield is only a temporary illusion.
The Bazaar experience is remembered as acceleration without mana.

Players remember Bazaar games by their strange rhythm. The Dredge pilot may keep a hand of three or four cards with confidence, perform most of the turn during upkeep and draw replacement, and cast almost nothing. The opponent may begin the game with Leyline of the Void already on the battlefield. Both players make mulligan decisions based on a land that has not yet been played.

They also remember the transformation of reputation. An effect that once appeared to be a bad deal became one of the most expensive and strategically central lands in the game. Bazaar is the classic reminder that Magic cards are not finished when printed; later mechanics can rewrite what old words mean.

XXVI · The Legacy

Why Bazaar of Baghdad still matters

Bazaar of Baghdad matters because it is the purest example of a card whose drawback became its engine. It loses a card, loses a mana source, and asks for a deck constructed around an unusual zone. Every one of those negatives is visible. None prevents it from defining a major branch of Vintage.

It taught players to distinguish cards in hand from usable resources, to evaluate replacement effects rather than printed verbs, and to build mulligan systems around a single permanent. It forced sideboards to respect the graveyard and encouraged designs that interact without mana. It gave Reanimator a loading dock, Dragon a route through the deck, Dredge an ignition switch, and Survival a market for recursive creatures.

Library of Alexandria rewards possession. Bazaar rewards circulation. One keeps seven cards and grows richer; the other throws cards away until “away” no longer exists. Together, the two lands from Arabian Nights describe two opposite theories of advantage—and both proved strong enough to shape the game for decades.

Bazaar’s paradox
The card is nominally negative card advantage and does not make mana. Its best decks make both facts irrelevant by treating the graveyard as a second hand and using alternative costs.
