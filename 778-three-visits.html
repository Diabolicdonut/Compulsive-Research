I · Opening hand

A Second Copy of the Untapped Forest Tutor

Three Visits is easiest to understand when the card is treated as a decision machine rather than a reputation. Its printed object combines sorcery structure with an emphasis on tutoring. The important question is not merely whether the card is powerful. It is what sequence, resource exchange, and deck construction make that power reliable.

The core operation is turning deck construction into a menu of situational answers and payoffs. That operation can look modest in isolation because Magic cards are not played in isolation. Mana development, combat, hidden information, opposing interaction, and the value of future draw steps all change what the same line of text is worth. A useful history therefore begins with the card as played, not with a ranking or a slogan.

This dossier records the stable facts first: Three Visits appeared in Portal Three Kingdoms in 1999, carries the type line Sorcery, and is credited to Qu Xin in its original printing. From there, the article follows the card through rules texture, deck-building demands, sequencing, counterplay, comparison, and material print history. Where tournament documentation has not been independently verified, the deck archive uses transparent editorial study shells instead of invented registrations.

II · First printing

The original object and its constraints

The original Portal Three Kingdoms printing fixed three things at once: a mana cost of {1}{G}, a mana value of 2.0, and the exact rules package reproduced above. Those facts establish the card’s floor before metagames and memories add interpretation. A lower cost changes how early the object can enter play; a higher cost increases the amount of protection or setup a deck may need. Neither number alone determines quality, but both determine the kinds of turns on which the card can participate.

Type line matters just as much. As a Sorcery, the card enters a web of rules that controls when it can be cast, what zones and removal effects can reach it, whether it can attack or block, and what other cards can search for or reuse it. The card’s identity is therefore partly printed text and partly the general rules carried by its types. This is why apparently concise designs can create long strategic arguments.

The original environment is not reconstructed here by invented anecdotes. The defensible claim is narrower: once Three Visits entered the card pool in 1999, deck builders could test turning deck construction into a menu of situational answers and payoffs at this exact rate and with this exact object type. Later card pools supplied different companions and opponents, but they did not erase the baseline established by the first printing.

Comparison clarifies the baseline. Nature's Lore and Farseek occupy adjacent design territory, yet each changes at least one of cost, timing, card type, targeting restriction, or payoff. Reading those differences prevents the archive from collapsing every card with a similar purpose into the same story.

III · Rules text

Read every verb, cost, and timing window

The Oracle text of Three Visits should be parsed in order. Costs are paid before effects happen. Targets, if any, are chosen when the spell or ability is put on the stack. Conditional clauses determine whether the promised exchange occurs, while duration language determines how long a bonus, restriction, or permission survives. These are elementary rules, but the card’s practical ceiling depends on respecting them.

The listed mechanical themes—tutoring—describe the card’s jobs, not shortcuts around its wording. A card that produces mana still requires a plan for spending it. A card that removes something still depends on legal targets and the opponent’s response window. A card that creates material still asks whether that material can be converted into damage, cards, mana, or protection before the battlefield changes.

Players often lose value by treating the most visible sentence as the whole card. The hidden text includes the cost of holding mana open, the information revealed by a chosen target, the opportunity cost of using an ability now rather than later, and the possibility that an opponent can answer the enabling permanent instead of the payoff. Exact play begins by naming those hidden costs.

Cultivate offers a useful comparison because its text reaches a related strategic destination through a different rules path. The lesson is not that the cards are interchangeable. It is that timing words, zone changes, and target restrictions are part of power, even when deck summaries reduce both cards to the same role label.

IV · Resource economy

What the exchange is actually worth

Every use of Three Visits can be described as an exchange among cards, mana, time, life, board position, and information. The printed effect may directly name only one or two of those resources, but the others remain present. Spending a card and mana now may protect several future draw steps. Declining to act may preserve flexibility but allow the opponent to improve their board. The right evaluation is situational rather than absolute.

The card’s strongest turns usually compress two tasks into one decision. A threat that also protects a resource, an answer that also advances a clock, or an engine that also stabilizes the board reduces the number of separate cards a deck must draw. Conversely, a use that accomplishes only the smallest printed task can leave the controller behind even when the effect technically resolves.

Tempo is especially easy to misread. Mana value 2.0 tells us the printed investment, but not the full turn cost. Colored requirements affect the mana base. Sorcery-speed commitment may expose a player to a counterattack. Holding the card for a perfect target can waste mana that might have advanced another plan. Good play asks which resource is scarce on this turn, not which mode looks most impressive in a vacuum.

Infernal Tutor and Attune with Aether help frame the same exchange from other angles. One may ask for more mana but offer a broader result; the other may be narrower yet easier to sequence. The archive uses such comparisons to identify the essential bargain without pretending that a card has one fixed numerical value in every game.

V · Construction

What a deck must provide

A deck that includes Three Visits must do more than make the mana cost legal. It must decide whether the card is an engine, an answer, a threat, a bridge between phases of the game, or a redundant copy of an effect the deck already wants. Those roles imply different copy counts. Engines reward early access but can be poor in multiples. Reactive cards depend on the expected targets. Finishers require enough setup to survive until their turn arrives.

The mana base should be built from actual colored requirements rather than total color count. A spell needed on curve may demand more sources than a later splash card with the same symbols. Lands also carry opportunity cost: tapped lands slow interaction, utility lands reduce colored consistency, and greedy fixing may expose the deck to nonbasic-land pressure. The printed cost of Three Visits is therefore a deck-building instruction as well as a rules fact.

Support cards should cover failure states rather than merely imitate the best-case scenario. If Three Visits needs a battlefield, include ways to establish one. If it is vulnerable to a particular answer, decide whether protection, recursion, redundancy, or speed is the better response. If it is strongest after an exchange, include cards that force that exchange. Synergy is most durable when the surrounding deck remains functional without drawing the namesake card.

Adjacent archive cards such as Nature's Lore, Cultivate, and Green Sun's Zenith illustrate three common construction choices: redundancy, complement, and alternate angle. Redundancy increases consistency; complement covers a weakness; an alternate angle punishes opponents for sideboarding too narrowly. The best package is the one that improves the deck’s total decision tree rather than maximizing a single screenshot-worthy turn.

VI · Sequencing

The turn before the famous turn

The decisive play with Three Visits is often prepared one turn earlier. A land was fetched to preserve the right color. A disposable permanent was held rather than traded. An opponent was allowed to resolve a minor spell so that interaction remained available for the true bottleneck. These choices are difficult to notice because the payoff card receives the visual attention while preparation looks like ordinary housekeeping.

Before casting the card, count available mana on both sides, identify likely instant-speed responses, and decide what happens if the first plan fails. This does not require perfect knowledge. It requires a branch: if the opponent answers now, which resource did they spend and what becomes possible next? If they decline, what follow-up converts the resolved card into an advantage before their next untap?

After resolution, avoid automatic activation. Permanents with choices are most powerful when their controller values the future option as part of the current board. The obvious mode may create immediate material, while a quieter mode protects against a sweep, preserves mana, or changes combat math. Even a straightforward spell can create a post-resolution choice about targets, attacks, and which resource to expose.

The comparison with Farseek is useful here because two cards can share a role while demanding opposite sequencing. One rewards deployment into an empty window; another rewards patience until a target appears. Role labels help with deck selection, but timing texture determines actual play.

VII · Counterplay

How opponents reduce the ceiling

Counterplay begins by separating the card from the conditions that make it exceptional. Sometimes the efficient answer is the card itself. In other games, the better target is the mana source, token maker, graveyard enabler, protection spell, or payoff that turns a fair exchange into an engine. The opponent should attack the narrowest structural point rather than the most famous permanent.

Timing matters. Answering before an ability resolves can prevent value; answering afterward may still be correct if it removes a repeated source of advantage. Leaving mana untapped can alter the controller’s sequence even when no answer is held. Conversely, representing too much can give the Three Visits player free time to develop a second plan. Good counterplay balances actual interaction with the cost of bluffing it.

Sideboarding should identify which part of the card matters in the matchup. Graveyard hate is weak if the deck can win as an ordinary midrange strategy. Narrow removal is poor if the card’s value is already earned on entry. Expensive universal answers may solve the object but lose the mana exchange. The best sideboard card often changes several matchups while still reaching the relevant permanent or zone.

Infernal Tutor demonstrates a different failure mode within related territory, while Attune with Aether shows another kind of resilience. Studying both helps an opponent avoid the mistake of using one answer pattern against every card that shares a broad strategic label.

VIII · Formats

The same text under different clocks

Formats change the value of Three Visits without changing its Oracle text. Faster environments punish setup and reward low opportunity cost. Slower environments increase the value of repeatable engines and flexible topdecks. Larger card pools supply stronger combinations but also stronger answers. Multiplayer adds more opponents, more total resources, and a political cost to becoming the visible leader.

The right question is therefore not whether the card is “a Modern card,” “a Commander card,” or “a Standard card” in the abstract. The question is what job it performs in a specific deck under a specific clock. A card can be central in one shell, a sideboard tool in another, and a nostalgic but inefficient option in a third. Those differences are evidence of context, not contradictions.

Legality and metagame position can change, so this archive does not freeze current format claims into prose. Before building for sanctioned play, readers should check the official format policy and the card’s current legality. The stable historical object remains the printed cost, type, text, and release; competitive status is a moving layer placed on top.

Comparisons to Nature's Lore, Farseek, and Cultivate show how card-pool scale changes evaluation. A newer or broader environment may offer cleaner redundancy. An older or narrower environment may make a distinctive effect uniquely important. Both situations can explain influence without requiring the card to remain optimal forever.

IX · Memory

Why the card remains easy to remember

Cards endure in player memory for more than win rate. Some create a recognizable turn pattern. Some change how hands are mulliganed or how opponents leave mana open. Some make a deck’s plan legible from a single battlefield object. Three Visits belongs in an archive because its combination of tutoring and sorcery structure gives players a compact story about what a deck is trying to do.

That story can survive even after the card’s competitive share falls. Later designs may improve the rate, divide the effect across new card types, or attach the same idea to a different color. The older card remains useful as vocabulary. Players can point to it when explaining a family of decisions, and designers can vary its constraints while preserving the recognizable question.

Material history reinforces that memory. The archive currently identifies 15 English paper printing record(s) sharing the same Oracle identity in the source export, with up to twelve shown below and distinct illustration records prioritized. Different frames and artworks change the object’s emotional tone even when the rules remain stable.

The original credit to Qu Xin is therefore not decorative metadata. It records the first visual form through which players encountered the card. Rules create strategic memory; image, frame, set symbol, and physical handling create object memory. A preservation project needs both.

XI · Practical reading

A representative decision tree

Imagine a game in which Three Visits is available, the opponent can plausibly interact, and both players have competing uses for their mana. The first branch is whether casting the card now improves the board enough to justify exposing it. The second is whether waiting gains information or merely gives the opponent a cleaner development turn. The third is what resource the controller is willing to lose if the answer arrives.

On the proactive branch, the controller casts the card and immediately plans the next conversion: attack, activate, sacrifice, recur, protect, or spend newly created mana. On the patient branch, the controller advances with another card while preserving Three Visits as a later pivot. Neither branch is automatically correct. The choice depends on hand texture, known information, and whether the opponent’s likely answer is more valuable than the card being exposed.

Now reverse the seat. The opponent asks whether a direct answer is necessary, whether the enabling resource can be attacked, and whether racing is more efficient than interaction. A player who answers every famous card on sight can be manipulated into poor exchanges. A player who refuses to respect an engine can lose several turns to one unresolved object. The skill is identifying the turn on which the card crosses from possibility into inevitability.

Attune with Aether supplies a useful alternate branch because it asks a similar strategic question at a different rate or with a different vulnerability. Comparing the two makes the decision tree concrete: which card is better when behind, which is safer when ahead, and which leaves more useful play when the first plan is answered?

XII · Closing argument

Preserve the question, not only the result

The lasting value of Three Visits is the question it repeatedly asks: how much is turning deck construction into a menu of situational answers and payoffs worth at this cost, on this card type, and at this point in the game? That question can be answered differently by different formats and eras without making the archive incoherent. Influence is often the persistence of the question rather than permanent dominance of one answer.

A complete reading therefore resists two simplifications. The first is nostalgia, which treats remembered success as proof that the card remains universally efficient. The second is presentism, which treats later competition as proof that the earlier design no longer matters. The historical object sits between them. It established a playable rate, taught a sequence, and became a comparison point for later cards.

The related cards discussed throughout this dossier—Nature's Lore, Farseek, Cultivate, Infernal Tutor, and Attune with Aether—do not replace Three Visits. They map the design space around it. Some preserve the role while changing the cost. Others preserve the timing while changing the payoff. Together they show why card history is best written as a network of decisions rather than a list of isolated rankings.

For play, the practical conclusion is equally direct: build the surrounding deck to make the ordinary case acceptable, sequence for the opponent’s real answers, and treat the spectacular case as upside rather than entitlement. That discipline is what turns a famous card from a story into a tool.
