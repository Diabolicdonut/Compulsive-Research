I · Opening hand

The Four-Mana Threat That Made Every End Step Dangerous

Original object · Rise of the Eldrazi

Splinter Twin

Enchantment — Aura

Enchant creature Enchanted creature has "{T}: Create a token that's a copy of this creature, except it has haste. Exile that token at the beginning of the next end step."

Original setRise of the Eldrazi · 2010
Mana cost{2}{R}{R}
Mana value4.0
Card classEnchantment — Aura

Splinter Twin was printed in Rise of the Eldrazi as a strange Aura for creatures with useful tap abilities. A year later, Deceiver Exarch gave it a deterministic kill: enchant the Exarch, tap it to create a hasty copy, use the copy’s enters-the-battlefield trigger to untap the original, and repeat until enough attackers exist.

The combo was powerful, but the deck’s historical importance came from the threat rather than the execution. A Twin player could pass with three mana open, cast a flash creature at the opponent’s end step, and demand an answer. If the opponent held mana back, the Twin player could simply draw cards, cast removal, or attack with ordinary creatures.

This created the “Twin tax.” Every land tapped on the wrong turn might mean death, yet preserving interaction each turn allowed the Twin deck to win a slower game. The combo therefore disciplined Modern around instant-speed answers and careful end-step sequencing.

Wizards banned Splinter Twin in January 2016, citing its sustained presence and the desire for competitive diversity. The card left the format, but the archetype’s play patterns remain a reference point whenever Modern discusses turn-four combo, flash threats, and the value of forcing opponents to keep mana open.

II · Origin

An Eldrazi-set Aura found its partner one year later

Rise of the Eldrazi contained many creatures with tap abilities and a slower limited environment, making Splinter Twin look like a value engine. Copying a creature once per turn could duplicate an enters-the-battlefield trigger or create an expendable attacker.

New Phyrexia’s Deceiver Exarch changed the card completely. The Exarch could flash in, untap a permanent, survive Lightning Bolt with four toughness, and then untap the original enchanted creature every time a token entered.

Pestermite supplied redundancy at three toughness, while Kiki-Jiki, Mirror Breaker offered additional copies of the creature-copying effect. The finished deck was not built around one fragile two-card pair; it contained overlapping pieces and a normal blue-red interaction shell.

III · Rules

How the infinite loop actually works

Splinter Twin grants the enchanted creature an activated ability: tap to create a token copy with haste, then exile that token at the beginning of the next end step.

With Deceiver Exarch or Pestermite, each token’s enters-the-battlefield trigger untaps the original enchanted creature. The original taps again for another token, producing any finite number chosen by the controller.

The tokens have haste, so they can attack immediately. The original creature usually entered on the opponent’s end step, allowing Splinter Twin to be cast in the controller’s main phase without waiting another turn.

Removing the creature in response to the Aura is the cleanest answer because the Aura fails to resolve. After the Aura resolves, removal can still interrupt the first activation or an untap trigger, but the controller may respond by creating additional tokens when priority allows.

IV · Threat economy

The combo’s best resource was untapped mana

Twin did not need to hold both combo pieces openly. Simply representing Deceiver Exarch made every opponent’s end step a decision. Tapping out for a threat could lose immediately; declining to act gave the Twin player time.

Remand, Lightning Bolt, Serum Visions, Snapcaster Mage, and Vendilion Clique ensured that waiting was not neutral. The deck converted the opponent’s caution into tempo and card selection.

This is why sorcery-speed hate often disappointed. A powerful answer in hand did not matter if the opponent had to tap out to cast it. Twin rewarded cheap interaction that could be deployed while advancing a board or preserving another response.

V · Tournament history

Modern learned the combo at its first Pro Tour

Samuele Estratti won Pro Tour Philadelphia in 2011 with Splinter Twin, immediately establishing the combo in Modern’s first major professional showcase. His list used Ponder and Preordain before those cantrips were banned, along with Pact of Negation and Kiki-Jiki redundancy.

Patrick Dickmann’s Grand Prix Antwerp victory in 2013 showed the archetype’s evolution into Tempo Twin. Snapcaster Mage, Vendilion Clique, Grim Lavamancer, and burn allowed the deck to win without assembling the Aura combo.

Antonio del Moral León won Pro Tour Fate Reforged in 2015 with a streamlined blue-red list. Twin remained one of Modern’s most visible successful decks even after years of metagame adaptation.

Alex Bianchi’s Jeskai Twin victory at Grand Prix Pittsburgh late in 2015 demonstrated the archetype’s breadth. Restoration Angel, Path to Exile, and Celestial Colonnade supported a control plan only weeks before the January 2016 ban.

VI · Construction

Eight creatures, four Auras, and a real deck around them

The basic package used four Splinter Twin and a mixture of Deceiver Exarch and Pestermite. More combo pieces increased turn-four kills but produced weak draws when the opponent overloaded on removal.

Tempo versions reduced combo density and added Snapcaster Mage, Tarmogoyf, or Vendilion Clique. Control versions splashed white for Path to Exile and Restoration Angel. The combo’s compactness allowed these changes without abandoning the threat.

The mana base needed untapped blue on turn three and double red on turn four while leaving room for utility lands. That requirement constrained splashes and made Blood Moon both a sideboard weapon and a card the deck could often exploit better than opponents.

Sideboards frequently transformed the deck further, removing some Auras against heavy interaction and becoming a fair blue-red control deck after the opponent drew narrow hate.

VII · Sequencing

The combo begins on the opponent’s turn

The classic sequence is to pass turn three with mana open, cast Deceiver Exarch at the opponent’s end step, tap or untap the relevant permanent, untap, and cast Splinter Twin with protection available if possible.

Do not expose the creature automatically. If the opponent leaves removal mana open, use the end step for a cantrip, Snapcaster Mage, or Vendilion Clique instead. The threat of the combo has already gained value by constraining them.

When casting the Aura, announce the target and allow responses. If the opponent removes the creature, the Aura is countered by the rules. Protection spells are often best saved for this exchange rather than spent on earlier minor threats.

Once the loop begins, demonstrate a finite number of iterations rather than physically creating each token. Competitive shortcuts still require both players to understand the repeated actions and the point at which an opponent intends to respond.

VIII · Counterplay

Interact at two points and keep mana honest

Creature removal in response to Splinter Twin is the most efficient answer. One spell trades for both the creature and the Aura because the Aura never resolves.

Instant-speed enchantment removal can answer the Aura after resolution, but the Twin player may create tokens in response if the creature is untapped. Effects that stop activated abilities, such as Pithing Needle naming the creature? Pithing Needle cannot name a token’s granted ability usefully by creature name only if the permanent shares that name; more reliable options include Spellskite, Torpor Orb for the untap trigger, or broad removal.

Countermagic attacks the expensive half, while discard can remove whichever piece the hand lacks redundancy for. The best hate is cheap enough to deploy without abandoning the rest of the game.

Pressure matters. A Twin player forced to use Deceiver Exarch as a blocker or spend Bolts defensively may lose the time needed to sculpt protection. The goal is to preserve interaction without giving unlimited turns.

IX · Format migration

A Modern archetype frozen in 2016

Splinter Twin is banned in Modern as of this review. The January 2016 announcement argued that its persistent presence reduced competitive diversity and that similar blue-red or Jeskai shells could continue without the card.

Legacy can legally play the combo, but the Aura is slow relative to the format’s interaction and combo engines. Commander uses it more often as one half of creature-copy combinations, especially with Kiki-Jiki-style creatures.

Pioneer does not include Rise of the Eldrazi, so the original combo is absent. Historic and other digital formats depend on their own card pools and policy lists.

Calls to unban Twin recur because the deck was interactive and skill-testing, but nostalgia and current balance are different questions. The approved article records the history without claiming a present-day unban would be safe.

X · Art and memory

The red Aura became an icon of blue permission

Goran Josic’s art shows a figure multiplied by red magical strands, but tournament memory usually associates the card with Islands left untapped. The contradiction captures the deck: the red enchantment won, while blue uncertainty made it possible.

“Respect the Twin” became strategic shorthand. Players counted the opponent’s third land, watched end steps, and declined to tap out even before seeing a combo piece.

The archetype also became a model for combo-control design. A compact win condition can improve interactive games when it forces meaningful timing decisions—but can reduce diversity when it becomes the default best shell for those decisions.

XI · Design echo

The two-card kill returned through planeswalker loyalty

Saheeli Rai and Felidar Guardian recreated the copy-and-untap loop in Standard years after Twin left Modern.

A comparative echo

Saheeli Rai

The planeswalker version used a loyalty ability and a permanent that blinked Saheeli, producing hasty Guardian copies. It proved so efficient that Felidar Guardian was banned in Standard.

The echo confirms that the danger lies not in one Aura but in compact copy engines whose pieces are useful enough to support a normal game.

XII · Decision point

The opponent passes with three mana

You control four lands and a strong sorcery-speed threat. The Twin opponent has three untapped lands and four cards. Casting your threat may win a long game, but it opens the end-step Exarch into Splinter Twin line.

Passing preserves removal but allows the opponent to cast a cantrip or Vendilion Clique and improve their hand. Casting a cheaper spell while leaving one mana open may split the difference, but only if the answer actually stops the combo.

The correct choice depends on known cards, life totals, and clock. The important point is that Twin created this decision without spending a card—the possibility of the combo was itself an active resource.

XIII · Legacy

Modern still speaks Twin’s language

Splinter Twin defined the standard for a turn-four combo that could inhabit an interactive deck. Its victories were not only games where the loop resolved; many came from opponents playing cautiously enough for ordinary creatures and spells to take over.

The ban removed the card but not the strategic category. Later decks are compared to Twin whenever they threaten an instant-speed kill while maintaining a fair backup plan.

That vocabulary is the card’s enduring legacy: a combo piece became a theory of how untapped mana controls a game.
