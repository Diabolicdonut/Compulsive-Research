The Enchantment That Made Every Spell Ask Permission

From Pro Tour Valencia to the long reign of Legacy Miracles, Counterbalance turned the top card of a library into repeatable permission—and taught both players to fight over mana values, information, and time.

I · Opening hand

The Enchantment That Made Every Spell Ask Permission

Counterbalance is a two-mana enchantment with no activated ability, no immediate card draw, and no guarantee that it will counter anything. Nevertheless, for more than a decade it helped define what permission could look like in Extended and Legacy. Instead of keeping mana untapped and matching one answer to one threat, its controller turned the top card of the library into a standing test: does the card above the deck have the same mana value as the spell on the stack?

That question is weak when the top card is unknown and oppressive when the top card can be arranged. Sensei’s Divining Top supplied the missing control. Brainstorm, Ponder, fetch lands, and later the miracle mechanic added further ways to shape the top of the deck. A two-card engine thereby converted library manipulation—normally a consistency tool—into repeatable countermagic that could function while the controller spent mana elsewhere.

Counterbalance’s history is therefore not the history of a lone enchantment. It is the history of an ecosystem: mana-value curves built to cover an opposing format, card-selection spells chosen for tactical timing rather than only smoothing, and opponents learning to bait triggers, attack the manipulation engine, or present threats at mana values the deck could not reliably reveal.

The card’s greatest strength was also the source of its reputation. Once Top and Counterbalance were both active, the opponent could not evaluate a spell from the cards visible in hand alone. Every draw step, every Top activation, every fetch land, and every card already known from a previous reveal changed the probability that the next spell would resolve.

The card

Counterbalance

{U}{U}
Enchantment

Whenever an opponent casts a spell, you may reveal the top card of your library. If you do and that card has the same mana value as that spell, counter that spell.

First releaseColdsnap · 2006

II · Coldsnap

A strange enchantment waiting for the right instrument

Counterbalance arrived in Coldsnap in 2006, a set presented as the long-lost third expansion of the Ice Age block. Its wording was compact and unusual: whenever an opponent casts a spell, the controller may reveal the top card of their library, and a matching mana value counters the spell. The enchantment did not ask for mana after it resolved, but it demanded information and control over a zone players normally treat as uncertain.

On its own, Counterbalance resembled a probabilistic annoyance. Constructed decks cluster around cheap spells, so blind reveals sometimes worked, but a random two-drop on top could not protect against a three-drop, and lands revealed for mana value zero usually did nothing. The design’s real ceiling emerged because Magic already contained inexpensive ways to inspect and reorder the library. Sensei’s Divining Top, printed two years earlier in Champions of Kamigawa, was the decisive partner.

The pairing was elegant because both halves retained independent utility. Top filtered draws and protected itself by moving onto the library. Counterbalance sometimes countered spells without help. Together they formed a feedback loop: the opponent cast a spell, Counterbalance triggered, and Top could be activated before that trigger resolved to choose among three possible mana values. For one-mana spells, Top itself could tap to draw a card and then sit on top of the library with mana value one.

The engine was not a literal hard lock. It was a coverage system whose reliability depended on the controller’s curve, available mana, known top cards, and the opponent’s spell costs. Understanding that distinction explains both why skilled play mattered and why the card could feel inevitable once a game had progressed long enough.

III · Rules engine

The trigger creates a window, not a prediction

Counterbalance triggers when an opponent casts a spell. The ability goes on the stack above that spell. The controller does not choose whether to reveal until the trigger resolves, and the ability does not target. This matters because the controller can respond to the trigger with Sensei’s Divining Top, Brainstorm, a fetch land, or another effect that changes the top card before the comparison occurs.

The comparison uses mana value. A normal card’s mana value is derived from the mana symbols in its upper-right corner, not the amount actually paid. Alternate costs, cost reductions, delve, affinity, and additional costs change payment without changing mana value. A Force of Will cast for its alternate cost still has mana value five; a Treasure Cruise cast for one blue mana still has mana value eight.

An X spell uses the chosen value of X while it is on the stack. A Chalice-style or Engineered Explosives-style spell can therefore occupy a mana value that differs from the printed non-X symbols alone. A face-down spell on the stack has mana value zero, which means a land or another zero-mana card on top can counter it. Split cards use the rules appropriate to their zone: in the library their characteristics can produce a combined mana value, while the half cast on the stack has the mana value of the spell actually chosen.

Revealing is optional. Declining to reveal preserves information and may prevent the opponent from learning a card the controller intends to draw. The obvious activation is not always correct: if the spell is harmless, allowing it to resolve may protect a known top card for a more important spell later in the turn.

IV · Coverage

A mana curve becomes a permission map

A Counterbalance deck is built with two curves in mind. The first is its ordinary functional curve: enough cheap cards to operate efficiently. The second is the opponent-facing distribution of mana values it can reveal. In Legacy, one and two have historically been the most important bands because cantrips, removal, fast mana, combo pieces, and efficient threats concentrate there. Three matters for cards such as Show and Tell, Knight of the Reliquary, and planeswalker-adjacent threats; four and above are harder to cover without specific planning.

Blind Counterbalance is therefore not random in the ordinary sense. A deck with many one-mana cantrips and removal spells will naturally hit a meaningful share of one-drops. The enchantment becomes far more exact when Top presents three choices, Brainstorm places cards from hand on top, or a fetch land discards an unwanted arrangement and produces a fresh three-card look.

The controller must also manage information over time. If Top has revealed a one, two, and land, then a spell at mana value three cannot be stopped by those cards unless another manipulation effect intervenes. If the opponent has already seen a Terminus returned to the top by Brainstorm, they may deliberately lead with a six-mana spell or wait until the known card is drawn. Counterbalance games reward memory because both players are building an incomplete map of the library’s surface.

This is why the engine was more than a pair of permanents. Its actual resource was the number of top-card transformations available before a relevant trigger resolved. Mana, fetch lands, cantrips, cards in hand, and known cards all contributed to that resource.

V · First breakthrough

Valencia proved the engine could win a Pro Tour

Rémi Fortier won Pro Tour Valencia in 2007 with an Extended deck commonly called CounterTop Goyf. The list played only two Counterbalances but four Chrome Mox, three Tops, three Trinket Mages, Dark Confidant, Tarmogoyf, and a toolbox of cheap artifacts. The engine was not yet the all-consuming control shell later associated with Legacy Miracles. It was one efficient component in a deck that could accelerate, draw cards, attack, steal opposing creatures, and lock out clusters of cheap spells.

That victory established several principles that would persist. Counterbalance did not require a creatureless control deck. A fast clock made every countered spell more consequential. Trinket Mage increased the effective number of Tops while finding Tormod’s Crypt, Engineered Explosives, or equipment. Chrome Mox let the deck place its enchantment ahead of the opponent’s curve. The lock was strongest when it was not the only plan.

Extended also showed the value of format-specific curve knowledge. Fortier’s deck could prepare for a field of efficient one- and two-mana spells while maintaining enough threes and fours to create uncertainty. Counterbalance was never equally good against everything; it was exceptional when the format compressed around predictable costs.

VI · Legacy

From CounterTop Goyf to Miracles

Legacy magnified every part of the engine. Brainstorm was a one-mana instant that could place an exact card on top in response to the trigger. Ponder increased consistency. Fetch lands turned Top’s three-card selection into repeated fresh looks. Force of Will protected the enchantment without requiring the controller to leave mana untapped. Tarmogoyf gave early CounterTop decks a short clock once the opponent’s cheap spells stopped resolving.

Gabriel Nassif’s Grand Prix Chicago victory in 2009 captured the mature “Next Level Blue” phase. His deck used Counterbalance and Top beside Dark Confidant, Tarmogoyf, Trygon Predator, Sower of Temptation, and Vedalken Shackles. The engine bought time, but the deck won through a web of individually powerful cards. Nassif’s own tournament report shows the human element: deciding whether to deploy Counterbalance or a threat, when to spend a manipulation effect, and which mana value mattered in a particular matchup.

The printing of miracle cards in 2012 transformed the shell. Terminus and Entreat the Angels rewarded a player for putting specific cards on top at specific times, exactly what Top and Brainstorm already did. Miracles could clear creatures for one mana, establish CounterTop, draw extra cards through Jace and Predict, and eventually win with angels or Monastery Mentor. Counterbalance became part of a control deck whose sweepers, finishers, and permission all drew strength from library manipulation.

By the middle of the 2010s, Miracles was the format’s reference control deck. Lists varied between Entreat, Mentor, Predict, legends packages, and red sideboard cards, but Counterbalance and Top remained the central engine. The question for other decks was no longer only whether they could remove an enchantment. It was whether they could stop the opponent from repeatedly rebuilding perfect top cards while also surviving Terminus and ordinary countermagic.

VII · Piloting

How to use Counterbalance

Know the opposing curve before the first trigger. Counterbalance decisions improve when the controller can name the cards that matter at each mana value. Against a tempo deck, one may represent cantrips and removal while two represents threats and permission. Against combo, the decisive band may be zero, one, or three. The correct Top arrangement is matchup-dependent.

Do not spend manipulation on irrelevant spells. A Top activation costs mana and may disrupt the draw step. Brainstorm and fetch lands are finite. If a spell does not advance the opponent’s route to victory, revealing blindly—or declining to reveal—can be better than consuming the only way to answer a later threat.

Sequence Counterbalance around protection. Casting it on turn two into open mana is powerful only when the opponent cannot remove it or exploit the tempo loss. Sometimes the correct line is cantrip first, protect with Force, or deploy a threat that forces the opponent to act into the future enchantment.

Use Top as both selector and card. With a one-mana spell on the stack, tapping Top to draw and place Top itself on the library creates a guaranteed mana-value-one reveal. That line changes the next draw and temporarily removes Top from the battlefield, so it should be used with awareness of what follows.

Build the deck’s curve intentionally. A Counterbalance list overloaded at one and two may dominate cheap formats but become porous against threes and fours. Sideboarding can change the coverage map. Leaving in an otherwise mediocre card can be defensible when its mana value is strategically essential.

VIII · Counterplay

How to fight the lock rather than the logo

The cleanest answers historically attacked the engine at an angle ordinary permission could not easily cover. Abrupt Decay could not be countered and destroyed either permanent. Krosan Grip’s split second prevented Top from escaping to the library and blocked most reactive manipulation. Pithing Needle shut off Top’s activated abilities. Cards cast through Cavern of Souls or other uncounterable effects ignored Counterbalance entirely.

When no direct answer is available, sequencing matters. Lead with a spell at a mana value the opponent is unlikely to cover, or bait a Top activation with a less important spell before casting the real threat. Multiple spells of different mana values in one turn can overload a single three-card arrangement. A fetch land used by the Counterbalance player may improve the top cards, but it also discards known information and can be forced at an awkward time.

Pressure is another form of disruption. CounterTop requires mana and time. A battlefield already capable of dealing lethal damage can make a perfect lock irrelevant. Creatures deployed before Counterbalance, manlands, flash threats, and resilient permanents force the control player to spend resources on survival rather than on maintaining universal coverage.

Finally, distinguish Counterbalance from the whole deck. Removing the enchantment does not defeat Jace, Terminus, Snapcaster Mage, or Monastery Mentor. Conversely, fighting every control card individually while leaving Top active may let the opponent rebuild Counterbalance. Counterplay succeeds by identifying which component is currently converting the most resources into inevitability.

IX · 2017

The Top ban ended the dominant form of Miracles

On April 24, 2017, Wizards banned Sensei’s Divining Top in Legacy. The announcement identified Miracles as the format’s best deck and described the repetitive Top activations that pushed matches toward time. Counterbalance itself remained legal. The intervention removed the card that made the enchantment reliable, protected itself, fixed draws, and enabled miracles with minimal opportunity cost.

The decision is important because it clarifies what regulators believed the problem to be. Counterbalance supplied the lock text, but Top supplied the repetition and consistency. Banning the enchantment would have removed one payoff while leaving Top’s logistical burden and other synergies intact. Banning Top dismantled the mature CounterTop system and forced Legacy control decks to find different foundations.

Counterbalance did not disappear completely. Brainstorm, Ponder, Predict, Scroll Rack-like effects, and later top-of-library tools could still support it. Yet without Top, the card became a metagame choice rather than the default center of Legacy control. Its afterlife demonstrates how dependent a famous card can be on a partner that turns a probabilistic effect into an operational engine.

X · Image and idea

Equilibrium made threatening

Counterbalance is not attached to a named character or a large story event. Its flavor is conceptual. The card depicts magical forces held against one another, and John Zeleznik’s composition emphasizes the suspended moment in which neither side has yet prevailed. That visual matches the game action: an opposing spell exists on the stack, but its future depends on whether the library can produce an equal weight.

The name is unusually exact. The controller is not simply countering a spell; they are balancing one mana value against another. The top of the library becomes a scale. When Top joined the card, that metaphor became literal gameplay: the player repeatedly adjusted the unseen side of the scale until the values matched.

Later players often remember Counterbalance through frustration—the hesitation before every spell, the Top activations, and the possibility that the opponent already has the needed value. Yet the design’s enduring interest comes from how much hidden-state reasoning it creates from one sentence. Both players are solving the same top-card puzzle with different information and different incentives.

XI · Legacy

A card that became archetype grammar

“CounterTop” became more than a deck name. It became shorthand for a class of engines in which selection and permission reinforce one another. “Counterbalance curve” became a meaningful deckbuilding phrase. Even players who never registered the card learned to count mana values, track known top cards, and ask whether a spell should be baited before the real action.

The card also illustrates why repeatable soft locks can be more oppressive than explicit hard locks. Counterbalance often left theoretical outs. The opponent could draw the right mana value, find removal, or sequence around the known top cards. The emotional pressure came from not knowing whether those outs were real, and from watching each attempted spell generate another decision window for the control player.

Its peak is over, but its influence remains visible whenever a deck converts top-of-library control into interaction. Counterbalance proved that card selection could be a defensive resource, that a mana curve could function as a counterspell inventory, and that a two-card engine could redefine how an entire format cast spells.
