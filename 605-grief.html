Entry 605 · The Free Discard Spell That Rewrote the Opening Hand

The First Turn Was Already Gone

Grief turned a black card into a zero-mana hand check. One-mana resurrection turned that exchange into two selected discards and a menacing creature, carrying the card from combo protection to a Pro Tour championship and finally to simultaneous Modern and Legacy bans.

I · Before the First Land

The game began with something missing

A normal discard spell asks for black mana, a card, and a turn in which the caster could have developed the battlefield instead. Grief asked for a black card and nothing else. The opponent revealed a hand before either player had necessarily played a land, and the Grief player selected the nonland card most capable of resisting what came next.

That exchange alone was powerful but comprehensible: two cards from the caster for one card from the opponent. The card became infamous because Modern already contained one-mana spells that returned a creature from the graveyard when it died. With the evoke sacrifice trigger waiting on the stack, Undying Malice, Feign Death, Malakir Rebirth, and later Not Dead After All could bring Grief back. The opponent lost a second card, and the caster kept a menacing creature—usually a 4/3 because the return spell added a +1/+1 counter.

Grief did not merely trade resources. It gave the player perfect information, removed the best answer, and let the rest of the opening turn proceed with the opponent already behind. Its name described the emotion of the card as precisely as its creature type described its mechanics.

II · A Set Built for Modern

Modern Horizons 2 skipped the safety rail

Modern Horizons 2 entered the format directly in June 2021 rather than passing through Standard. That freedom let the set speak in Modern’s existing vocabulary: pitch spells, graveyard engines, tribal callbacks, artifact synergies, and efficient threats. Grief was part of a five-card cycle of mythic Elemental Incarnations whose alternate costs asked the caster to exile a card of the appropriate color.

The cycle deliberately recalled Force of Will: trade an extra card for the right to act without mana. But each incarnation attached that transaction to a creature and an enters-the-battlefield ability. Solitude removed a creature, Subtlety delayed a creature or planeswalker, Fury divided damage, Endurance moved graveyards back into libraries, and Grief attacked the hand.

All five were designed to be cast for free in emergencies. Grief was the one most naturally aimed at the beginning of the game, because information and hand disruption are worth the most before the opponent has deployed anything.

III · The Ancestor

Unmask had already taught black to pitch a card

Grief’s closest ancestor was Unmask, the Mercadian Masques sorcery that could exile a black card instead of paying four mana. Unmask proved that black could receive a Force-of-Will-style interaction spell by paying with cardboard rather than life. Reanimator decks used it because the spell both protected a combo and left all available mana free for Entomb or Reanimate.

The pitch-discard lineage

Unmask without the creature

Grief reproduced Unmask’s alternate cost and targeted hand attack, but moved the effect onto an enters-the-battlefield trigger. That change created an entire second axis of abuse: blink, reanimation, sacrifice, and death-trigger manipulation.

A spell normally finishes resolving and goes to the graveyard. A creature arrives, triggers abilities, can be targeted, can die, and may return. The body was not a decorative bonus. It was the bridge that converted a fair two-for-one exchange into the play pattern that defined the card.

IV · Two Triggers

Evoke created a window between arrival and death

When Grief is cast for its evoke cost, it enters the battlefield and produces two triggered abilities: its discard trigger and the evoke ability instructing its controller to sacrifice it. The controller places those triggers on the stack in the order they choose. The important point is that Grief remains on the battlefield while both triggers wait.

1Exile a black card and cast Grief for its evoke cost.
2Grief enters; discard and sacrifice triggers are placed on the stack.
3Resolve the discard trigger, learning the opponent’s remaining hand.
4Before sacrifice resolves, cast a return or blink spell targeting Grief.
5Grief returns as a new object and triggers discard again.

The original evoke trigger still resolves, but it refers to the old Grief that left the battlefield. The returned creature is a new game object and is not sacrificed by that trigger. This is the technical heart of “Scam”: a rules-clean interaction whose result feels as though the game has cheated the ordinary accounting of mana and cards.

V · The Hidden Cost

Free did not mean free

Evoking Grief costs two cards: Grief itself and another black card. If the creature dies as intended, the caster has spent two cards to remove one. That is real card disadvantage. A deck that simply jammed Grief into an ordinary midrange list could exhaust itself, especially when the opponent’s hand contained redundant threats or cheap draw spells.

The best Grief decks converted the exiled black card from “payment” into deck construction. Rakdos lists were overwhelmingly black, Living End contained black cycling creatures and multicolored cascade cards, Reanimator naturally stocked black spells, and Goryo’s Vengeance decks had legendary creatures they did not intend to cast normally. A card was still lost, but the mana-free interaction was aligned with the deck’s larger plan.

Grief was strongest when the card exiled to it was less important than the turn it purchased.

That distinction explains why the card could be mediocre in one opening hand and oppressive in another. Evoke Grief without follow-up was an expensive form of insurance. Evoke Grief with a return spell, combo, or reanimation line was a complete opening strategy.

VI · The Nickname

“Scam” described the feeling, not an illegal action

The community nickname came from the apparent impossibility of the exchange. A player exiled one card, cast a creature without mana, took the opponent’s best card, brought the creature back for one mana, took another card, and attacked for four with menace on the following turn. Every step followed the rules. The result nevertheless felt like a confidence trick performed before normal Magic had started.

The return spells were previously narrow cards. Undying Evil and its descendants were designed to protect creatures from removal. Grief supplied its own death event, so protection became proactive development. The deck no longer waited for an opponent to kill something; it manufactured the death and collected the return trigger immediately.

The nickname eventually broadened. “Rakdos Scam” described the entire black-red midrange deck, even in games where it never executed the opening. That was important: the deck won a great many matches by playing Ragavan, Bowmasters, Fable, removal, and discard. The explosive start was the ceiling, not the only floor.

VII · The Opening Hand

The turn-one line attacked both choice and recovery

A single discard spell removes the opponent’s best card but leaves the rest of the hand intact. Double Grief was different because the first trigger provided information for the second. The Grief player could remove interaction first, then use the second trigger to take the card that made the remaining hand functional: a cascade spell, a payoff, a removal spell, or the only meaningful threat.

The opponent’s mulligan decisions made this harsher. A six-card hand exposed to two Grief triggers could begin its first turn with four cards, while the Grief player had already developed a creature. Even when the raw card counts were close, the distribution was not. The opponent lost selected cards; the caster chose which black card to exile and retained the cards designed to exploit the opening.

Menace mattered as well. A 4/3 with menace demanded two blockers, so a damaged hand could not easily stabilize with one cheap creature. The body turned the informational advantage into a clock.

VIII · First Homes

Grief spread before one shell claimed it

Early experimentation placed Grief in Reanimator, Living End, black midrange, Ephemerate shells, and decks built almost entirely around the undying interaction. Each home valued a different part of the card. Combo decks wanted a free safety check. Reanimator wanted both discard and a creature in the graveyard. Midrange wanted the repeated trigger and body. Blink decks wanted the enters-the-battlefield ability.

This flexibility made Grief difficult to evaluate by looking only at one archetype. Removing a return spell from Modern might weaken Rakdos but leave Living End untouched. Attacking graveyards hurt Reanimator but did little against a simple evoked copy that cleared countermagic. The card’s common function was proactive permission: before asking whether its own plan would work, the Grief deck inspected the opponent’s answer.

IX · Combo Protection

Living End turned discard into a future creature

Living End was one of Grief’s cleanest homes. The deck cycled creatures into the graveyard, cascaded into its namesake sorcery, swept away the opposing battlefield, and returned its own creatures. Grief could be evoked before the cascade turn to remove countermagic or graveyard hate, then return later with the rest of the team.

The card therefore violated its apparent two-for-one drawback. The Grief card itself was not permanently spent; it became one more reanimation target. The black card exiled to pay evoke often came from a deck dense with replaceable cyclers. Grief gave a linear combo deck the hand knowledge of a black midrange deck without requiring it to leave mana open.

X · Reanimation

The discard creature entered the graveyard exactly where it was wanted

In Reanimator, Grief inherited Unmask’s old job and added a body worth returning. A first trigger could remove Force of Will, Swords to Plowshares, or graveyard interaction. Then Reanimate could target Grief itself for a second discard trigger when the deck lacked a larger creature or when perfect information mattered more than immediate damage.

The life payment was trivial: Grief’s mana value was four, far less costly than returning an Atraxa or Griselbrand. In Legacy, that efficiency helped create Dimir Reanimator lists that behaved partly like tempo decks. They could disrupt, reanimate Grief, protect it with Daze and Force, and win without resolving the enormous creature normally associated with the archetype.

XI · The Midrange Shell

Rakdos made the high roll part of a real deck

The most successful construction lesson was not merely “play Grief and undying spells.” It was to surround that package with cards that remained excellent when drawn later. Ragavan, Nimble Pilferer punished opponents who stumbled. Dauthi Voidwalker attacked graveyards and stole spells. Fable of the Mirror-Breaker filtered away situational cards and generated material. Orcish Bowmasters punished card draw and supplied instant-speed pressure.

Thoughtseize created redundancy with Grief, while Fatal Push, Terminate, and later Molten Collapse handled the battlefield. The result could keep a hand with no scam line and still play one of Modern’s strongest fair games. That resilience separated a tournament archetype from a gimmick.

XII · Barcelona, 2023

Grief won a Modern Pro Tour

At Pro Tour The Lord of the Rings, Rakdos Evoke was the most represented archetype and Jake Beardsley carried it through the event to the championship. His list used four Grief, six one-mana return spells, four Fury, and the newly printed Orcish Bowmasters. The deck could begin with the famous double-discard turn, but its victory also demonstrated the strength of the full shell.

Beardsley’s Modern record was 11–1–1, and the archetype posted a positive aggregate win rate despite being the deck everyone expected to face. Grief had moved beyond feared ladder experience or theoretical non-game. It now sat at the center of the highest-profile Modern result of the season.

XIII · The Deck to Beat

Success converted resentment into a format question

After the Pro Tour, Rakdos Evoke became a reference point for deck construction. A Modern deck needed to survive an opening hand stripped of its two best nonland cards, answer a menacing creature, and still compete against an ordinary midrange draw. Sideboards had to respect graveyard-adjacent return spells without becoming too narrow against the rest of the field.

Counterplay existed. Leyline of Sanctity stopped the targeted trigger. Instant-speed graveyard exile could interrupt return spells. Cheap removal could answer the surviving body. Redundant engines reduced the value of selected discard. But much of that counterplay had to be present in the opening hand, and Grief itself revealed whether it was there.

This was the central experiential complaint: the card did not merely ask whether the opponent had an answer. It saw the answer and removed it.

XIV · December 2023

Fury left; Grief remained

When Wizards banned Fury from Modern in December 2023, many expected Rakdos Evoke to collapse. Fury had supplied free creature control, an equally potent undying interaction, and a way to dominate small-creature decks. The ban removed half of the deck’s Elemental identity.

Rakdos adapted. It became more explicitly a Grief deck, replacing free red removal with conventional interaction and leaning harder on Not Dead After All, Bowmasters, Fable, Dauthi Voidwalker, and Sheoldred. Mariluz García’s victory at a large Barcelona qualifier shortly after the ban showed that the archetype’s defining opening and fair-game structure remained intact.

XV · A Control Deck in Disguise

Esper Goryo’s used every face of the card

By the summer of 2024, Grief had become part of Esper Goryo’s Vengeance. Matt Sperling went undefeated in the Modern portion of Pro Tour Modern Horizons 3 with a list that could reanimate Atraxa or Griselbrand, blink creatures with Ephemerate, and use Grief as free protection.

Ephemerate created a particularly elegant line. Cast Grief for evoke, blink it before the sacrifice trigger, and it returns without needing to die. The spell generated another discard trigger and could rebound later for Solitude, Atraxa, or another Grief. The deck demonstrated that the card’s problem was not confined to the narrow undying package. Any cheap method of causing the creature to leave and return could exploit the same trigger structure.

XVI · The Legacy Merger

Dimir Reanimator absorbed the tempo deck

Legacy’s Grief decks gradually blurred archetype boundaries. Traditional Reanimator sought a fast giant creature; Delver-style tempo sought a cheap threat protected by free countermagic. Dimir Reanimator could do both. Grief and Troll of Khazad-dûm supplied black cards and reanimation targets. Brainstorm and Ponder found pieces. Daze, Force of Will, and Wasteland punished opponents trying to recover.

Psychic Frog strengthened the fair plan in 2024. A deck that once seemed all-in could now strip the hand, land a small threat, and play a long game. Grief was the hinge: combo protection when the deck moved quickly, a Reanimate target when the large creatures were unavailable, and a tempo threat when backed by countermagic.

XVII · August 26, 2024

Modern and Legacy reached the same verdict

Wizards of the Coast banned Grief in both Modern and Legacy on August 26, 2024. The Modern explanation focused on the frequency and quality of one-mana return effects, especially the turn-one sequence that stripped multiple cards and left a creature. The Legacy explanation focused on Grief plus Reanimate and the growth of Dimir Reanimator.

The simultaneous bans were unusual evidence of one design pattern stressing two very different formats. Modern had no Dark Ritual, Entomb, or Force of Will, yet its cheap return spells made the trigger reusable. Legacy had more powerful answers, but it also had one-mana reanimation and the blue protection needed to convert early discard into a locked-in advantage.

XVIII · After the Ban

The archetypes survived, but the opening changed

Living End replaced Grief with other protection and interaction. Reanimator continued to put enormous creatures into play but lost the free discard body that also served as a reanimation target. Rakdos midrange remained a collection of efficient black-red cards rather than a deck built around a one-mana resurrection trick.

The bans therefore did not erase the strategies. They removed the common mechanism that allowed each strategy to inspect and dismantle an opponent’s hand before committing. Games still ended quickly; they simply began with more concealed information and a greater chance that the defending player’s opening hand would remain functional.

XIX · Beyond the Bans

Vintage, Timeless, and Commander preserve different versions

Where legal, Grief is constrained by context. Vintage contains stronger pitch spells and faster engines, so a two-card discard exchange competes with the most efficient interaction ever printed. Timeless has access to powerful reanimation and hand disruption but exists within a digital card pool and its own restricted-list philosophy. Commander multiplies the number of opposing hands, making a targeted one-for-one effect less dominant than in a duel.

In multiplayer, Grief is often used as a surgical tool: remove a counterspell before a combo, protect a reanimation turn, or repeatedly blink the creature in a deck designed around enters-the-battlefield effects. The card remains potent, but taking one card from one of three opponents is not the same as dismantling half of a six-card Modern hand.

XX · Playing the Card

A Grief hand was judged by what followed the information

The most tempting opening contained Grief, a black card, and a return spell. That did not automatically make it a keep. The rest of the hand needed lands, pressure, or a coherent combo. A double-discard start followed by several dead cards could still lose to the top of the opponent’s library.

Conversely, hands without the return spell could use Grief as a safety valve. Against a fast combo deck, pitching two cards to remove the critical piece might buy enough time. Against a grindy opponent with redundant cards, evoking Grief fairly could be a trap. Expert play required distinguishing “free interaction” from “necessary interaction.”

XXI · Sequencing

The first discard determined the second

With two triggers available, sequencing depended on the opponent’s hand. The first discard usually removed the card capable of stopping the return spell or punishing the graveyard. After Grief returned, the second trigger could attack the hand’s plan. Against an unknown deck, the first reveal also identified the matchup before the Grief player chose a land, threat, or follow-up discard spell.

When Thoughtseize was also available, the Grief player could decide whether life, mana, or cards were the scarce resource. Thoughtseize first preserved the black card that would otherwise be exiled, while Grief first conserved mana and allowed the revealed hand to guide later choices. These decisions made the deck more demanding than the nickname implied.

XXII · Fighting Back

Redundancy was the best defense

The cleanest way to resist selected discard is to make no single card indispensable. Multiple lands, overlapping threats, and several copies of an engine reduce the value of Grief’s information. Cards that replace themselves or operate from the graveyard also make the exchange less final.

Reactive answers were awkward because Grief arrived before mana. Leyline of Sanctity could prevent targeting from the opening hand. Surgical Extraction or Endurance could sometimes interrupt a return line, depending on timing and format. Free countermagic could stop the resurrection spell, although the first discard trigger often removed it. Once the creature returned, cheap removal was still useful—but it did not restore the cards already taken.

XXIII · Rules Edges

The returned creature is a new Grief

A Grief that leaves the battlefield and returns has no memory of its previous existence. Counters, damage, attached Auras, and effects applying to the original object do not carry over unless a rule or spell specifically says otherwise. The old evoke trigger cannot sacrifice the new object.

If the discard target becomes illegal before the trigger resolves—for example, because that player gains hexproof—the trigger does nothing. If a player has no nonland card, the hand is still revealed but nothing is discarded. Because the ability targets an opponent rather than a card, the choice is made only as the trigger resolves.

Grief’s enters-the-battlefield ability triggers whether it was cast, reanimated, blinked, or otherwise put onto the battlefield. Evoke controls only how it may be cast and whether the sacrifice trigger occurs.

XXIV · The Image

Nicholas Gregory painted absence as a creature

The original Grief is less a conventional monster than a vertical wound in the scene. Its elongated body rises from darkness, framed by pale faces and reaching forms that suggest memory, mourning, or identities being pulled away. The composition makes the creature feel like the space left by something rather than the thing itself.

That visual idea matched the gameplay. Grief was recognized by what disappeared: the answer that never reached the battlefield, the combo piece that never resolved, the opening hand that no longer resembled the hand its owner kept. The alternate borderless illustration by Svetlin Velinov made the incarnation more physically monstrous, while later Special Guest and Secret Lair treatments interpreted the same emotional abstraction in different visual languages.

XXV · The Family

The Incarnations changed how “creature” was read

The five-card cycle made a creature card function as interaction even when it never survived. Each incarnation could be a spell on turn zero, a body later, a pitch card for another effect, or a reanimation target. Grief was the member whose trigger most directly enabled its own abuse, because removing interaction from the hand protected whatever spell would save or return it.

The cycle’s lasting design lesson was that enters-the-battlefield text can be more important than the printed body. Once a creature can be cast without mana, every blink or resurrection spell must be read as a potential copy of that text.

XXVI · The Versions

Grief accumulated variants faster than it accumulated years

The original Modern Horizons 2 release appeared in a regular frame with Nicholas Gregory artwork and a borderless version with inverted composition and art by Svetlin Velinov. Prerelease-stamped copies followed the main version. The retro-frame Modern Horizons 2 Timeshifts release returned the card to an older visual vocabulary after it had already been banned from Historic but before its paper-format bans.

Modern Horizons 3 Special Guests supplied Campbell White artwork in conventional and textured-foil forms. Magic Online received a promotional object, and a later Secret Lair interpretation by Anna Pavleeva extended the card’s visual life beyond the formats where it had become notorious.

XXVII · What Players Remember

The emotional history is part of the mechanical history

Some cards are remembered for decisions made on turn twelve. Grief is remembered for hands placed back on the table after the second discard trigger. Its games could still be complex, especially when Rakdos moved into a midrange exchange, but the defining image was immediate and personal: the player had selected an opening hand, and the opposing card selected which parts they were allowed to keep.

“Did they have Grief plus the return spell?” became a pregame question with the force of a matchup result. The sequence was memorable because it happened before either player had established an ordinary battlefield on which to fight back.

That reputation should not obscure the technical sophistication of its best decks or pilots. It does explain why banning discussions focused as much on play experience as on raw win percentage. Grief compressed information, interaction, and pressure into the earliest possible window.

XXVIII · The Verdict

Grief made the opening hand part of the battlefield

Magic has always allowed discard. Grief changed when discard could happen, what object carried it, and how cheaply the trigger could be repeated. The card forced players to treat the opening hand not as protected preparation but as a zone under immediate attack.

Its competitive life was brief by the standards of Eternal staples—just over three years before simultaneous Modern and Legacy bans—but dense with consequences. It protected combo, powered reanimation, won a Pro Tour in midrange, survived the loss of Fury, and ended as the common denominator between two formats’ most criticized openings.

Grief’s legacy is therefore larger than “a free Thoughtseize.” It is a demonstration of what happens when a pitch spell, an enters-the-battlefield trigger, and cheap recursion all occupy the same rules space. The mana cost disappeared first. Soon afterward, so did the opponent’s hand.
