The First Turn Was Already Gone

Grief turned a black card into a zero-mana hand check, then let blink and reanimation turn that exchange into a creature, a second discard trigger, and eventually simultaneous Modern and Legacy bans.

I · Before the first land

The First Turn Was Already Gone

Grief did not merely take a card. It took the opponent’s first decision before the game had properly begun.

A normal discard spell asks for black mana, a card in hand, and a turn in which the caster could have developed the battlefield instead. Grief asked for a black card and no mana at all. The opponent revealed a hand before either player had necessarily played a land, and the Grief player selected the nonland card most capable of resisting what came next.

On its own, that exchange was severe but comprehensible. The caster spent Grief and another black card to take one card from the opponent. The transaction was card disadvantage: two cards traded for one. Modern Horizons 2 appeared to have priced the freedom of a zero-mana discard spell by making it temporary and expensive in cardboard.

The problem was the word creature.

Because the discard effect belonged to an enters-the-battlefield ability, the brief life of the evoked creature became a resource. Ephemerate could blink it before the evoke sacrifice resolved. Undying Malice, Feign Death, Malakir Rebirth, and later Not Dead After All could let it die and return it. Each route produced another discard trigger and left a three-power menace creature behind.

The result was a play pattern that compressed information, disruption, tempo, and threat deployment into the opening turn. Grief looked like a free spell with a built-in safety valve. Competitive decks discovered that the valve was a doorway.

II · Modern Horizons 2

Modern Horizons 2 skipped the safety rail

Modern Horizons 2 was designed to enter Modern directly. It did not have to survive Standard, and it did not have to place its strongest cards behind the slower assumptions of a rotating environment. The set could speak directly to fetch lands, cascade, graveyard engines, pitch spells, and the deep card pool that already defined nonrotating play.

Grief was one member of a five-card cycle of Elemental Incarnations. Each could be cast without mana by exiling a card of its color. Each attached a familiar spell effect to a creature body: Solitude exiled a creature, Subtlety delayed a creature or planeswalker, Fury divided damage, Endurance recycled a graveyard, and Grief attacked the hand.

The cycle’s apparent balance came from loss. Pitching a card is real cost, and evoke ensures the creature is sacrificed after entering. In emergency use, the caster accepts card disadvantage to interact while tapped out. That structure resembles Force of Will: the free action is not meant to be efficient, merely available when the alternative is losing.

But the Incarnations were not instants. They were creatures whose abilities triggered after they arrived. That distinction allowed every ordinary creature-manipulation mechanic—blink, reanimation, sacrifice, death triggers, and recursion—to touch the free spell.

The safety valve was written on the card. The abuse came from treating the valve as another triggered ability.Modern Horizons 2 · 2021

Grief was the cycle member most naturally aimed at turn one. Creature removal becomes more valuable after threats exist; graveyard repair matters after cards have moved; a counter-like delay waits for a spell. Hand disruption is strongest before the opponent has deployed anything. The earlier Grief appeared, the more complete its information and the more damaging its choice.

III · The rules window

The window between arrival and death

When Grief is cast for its evoke cost, two triggered abilities matter after it enters the battlefield. One is the discard trigger. The other is the evoke trigger instructing its controller to sacrifice it. Because the same player controls both triggers, that player chooses the order in which they are placed on the stack.

The discard trigger can resolve first. The opponent reveals their hand, and the Grief player removes the card most likely to answer the follow-up. Before the sacrifice trigger resolves, the Grief player may cast a spell that moves Grief somewhere else.

Ephemerate exiles and returns it. The returning object is a new Grief, no longer connected to the original evoke trigger. It produces another discard trigger and remains on the battlefield. An undying-style spell works from the opposite direction: Grief is sacrificed, returns under its owner’s control, and triggers again.

The final exchange can be devastating. The Grief player spends Grief, a black card to pitch, and a one-mana return spell. The opponent loses two chosen nonland cards. The Grief player keeps a 3/2 menace creature and full knowledge of what remains.

That is not always raw card advantage. Depending on the exact sequence, both players may have spent similar numbers of cards. The imbalance is in selection and tempo. The Grief player chooses which cards disappear, preserves the threat, and begins attacking before the opponent has established mana.

Rules distinction. Returning Grief does not “cancel” the evoke trigger by countering it. The original Grief leaves the battlefield, and the returned Grief is a new object. When the old sacrifice trigger resolves, it cannot sacrifice the new object.

This was the mechanical core of the decks later called “Scam.” The nickname described the experience of seeing an apparently temporary creature become permanent, but every part of the line followed ordinary rules. The shock came from how much ordinary rules text could be compressed into the opening turn.

IV · Pitch-discard lineage

Unmask with a body

Grief did not invent free black discard. Unmask had done the conceptual work in Mercadian Masques more than twenty years earlier. Unmask could be cast by exiling a black card from hand rather than paying mana, and it allowed the caster to examine an opponent’s hand and remove a nonland card.

Mercadian Masques · 1999

Unmask

The difference is exactly the difference between a spell and a permanent. Unmask normally finishes resolving and goes to the graveyard. Grief enters the battlefield before its discard ability resolves. That arrival invites the rest of Magic’s card pool into the transaction.

Mercadian Masques · 1999

The free discard spell before the creature

Unmask established the pitch-discard exchange. Grief reproduced it on an enters-the-battlefield trigger, making blink, reanimation, sacrifice, and death matter.

The body did not merely add three power. It changed which categories of cards could interact with the effect.

Grief also inherited part of the cultural position of Thoughtseize. Both cards make the opponent reveal the structure of their game plan and allow the caster to take the most important piece. Thoughtseize is efficient and clean: one mana, one card, one discard. Grief was less efficient in isolation but more explosive when its body could be preserved.

This is why discussions that treated Grief as “free Thoughtseize” missed the most important fact. The card was not merely a discard spell that cost no mana. It was a discard spell attached to a creature whose temporary existence could be converted into another object.

V · The archetypes

From support spell to archetype

Grief first spread because several established strategies already wanted exactly what it offered.

Living End could pitch Grief for early interaction, then return it later with the rest of the deck’s cycling creatures. The initial card disadvantage became less meaningful because the cascade spell converted a stocked graveyard into a battlefield. Grief gave a linear combo deck perfect information and a way to clear countermagic before committing.

Reanimator strategies viewed the card from another angle. Evoke placed a creature into the graveyard without spending mana. Reanimate could bring it back for a single black mana, producing another discard trigger and a threat. In Legacy, that line was protected by Daze and Force of Will; the same deck could also use Grief to remove graveyard hate before attempting to reanimate something larger.

Rakdos decks made the card’s interaction the center rather than a supporting line. They paired Grief and Fury with eight or more one-mana spells that returned a creature after death. The opening high roll was frightening, but the shell’s longevity came from everything else: Thoughtseize, Ragavan, Dauthi Voidwalker, removal, and Fable of the Mirror-Breaker. A deck built around an explosive opening was still capable of playing midrange Magic for ten turns.

VI · Barcelona

A Pro Tour champion

Pro Tour The Lord of the Rings in July 2023 gave Grief its defining tournament image. Jake Beardsley reached the final with Rakdos Evoke in his first Pro Tour appearance. Across the table sat Christian Calcano on Mono-Green Tron, a deck built to assemble enormous mana rather than trade resources one card at a time.

The matchup dramatized Grief’s purpose. Tron’s opening hands often contain a delicate relationship among lands, search effects, and expensive payoffs. A chosen discard spell could remove the bridge between those pieces. A second trigger could take the next best route. The menace creature then began attacking while the Tron player tried to rebuild.

The fifth game began with Grief and Undying Malice. Beardsley removed Wurmcoil Engine and The One Ring, then followed with Blood Moon. Calcano’s deck was not merely slowed; its plan had been read, edited, and locked before it could produce green mana.

That victory mattered because it made Grief more than a controversial ladder experience or an online metagame complaint. The card had won one of competitive Magic’s highest stages inside a deck that was both explosive and structurally sound.

The deciding game opened with two chosen discards and ended with Grief still attacking.Pro Tour The Lord of the Rings · 2023

The championship also complicated the public argument. Rakdos Evoke was not unbeatable; the Pro Tour data did not show it overwhelming every matchup. Yet its most visible games displayed exactly the experience critics objected to: an opponent keeping a reasonable hand and discovering that its meaningful decisions had been removed before the first draw step could repair them.

VII · The verdict

The bans arrived together

Wizards first acted against the deck by banning Fury from Modern in December 2023. Fury was the better battlefield stabilizer and a major reason creature decks struggled against Rakdos Evoke. Grief remained legal, partly because it appeared less dominant when separated from the full Incarnation package.

The card did not disappear. Rakdos rebuilt around Not Dead After All and conventional black-red interaction. Living End kept it. Esper Goryo’s used it with Ephemerate, Solitude, Atraxa, and Goryo’s Vengeance. Mono-black strategies adopted it. The exact shell changed, but the opening sequence remained available wherever a deck could support enough black cards and one-mana recursion.

At Pro Tour Modern Horizons 3 in June 2024, Matt Sperling went 10–0 in the Modern rounds with Esper Goryo’s. Grief was not the namesake engine, but it protected the graveyard plan, interacted for no mana, and shared Ephemerate with the deck’s other creatures. The card had proved that banning Fury removed one deck configuration without removing the underlying interaction.

Legacy developed an even more compact version. Reanimate cost one mana, Animate Dead cost two, and blue countermagic protected the sequence. Grief could attack the opponent’s graveyard hate, enter the graveyard itself, and return as a threat. Dimir Reanimator gradually absorbed elements of tempo decks until it could win with Psychic Frog and Wasteland even when the graveyard plan was contained.

On August 26, 2024, Wizards banned Grief in both Modern and Legacy. The Modern explanation emphasized the experience of beginning down multiple chosen cards while facing a three- or four-power menace creature. The Legacy explanation emphasized that the format had failed to self-correct against Grief plus Reanimate and that the card attacked the hate intended to contain Reanimator.

The simultaneous bans were historically fitting. Modern and Legacy have different speeds, answers, and card pools, but Grief found the same pressure point in both: the opponent needed cards in hand to participate, and Grief was best at removing those cards before participation began.

Grief’s post-ban life clarifies what made Modern and Legacy unusual. The card is not universally too strong in every environment. In Commander, spending two cards to remove one opponent’s card is unattractive when two other opponents remain. In Vintage, free spells and broken mana are ordinary enough that Grief competes with more extreme openings.

Modern and Legacy sat in the exact middle where the card was both consistent enough to shape deck construction and disruptive enough to erase ordinary games. The ban was not a declaration that no format could contain it. It was a declaration that those two formats should not be required to.

IX · The image

The artwork and the object

Nicholas Gregory’s original Grief is not a conventional monster portrait. The figure appears partially hollow, its body assembled from elongated limbs, dark cavities, and pale surfaces that seem stretched around absence. The face is difficult to locate. The creature looks less like something approaching than something left after a person has been removed.

That visual ambiguity suits the card’s effect. Grief does not destroy a permanent already present. It edits possibility. The missing card never reaches the battlefield, and the opponent’s future becomes defined by an absence only both players can fully understand.

The borderless Modern Horizons 2 version, illustrated by Svetlin Velinov, turns the concept outward. The figure becomes more immediate and violent, framed as a looming force rather than a distant wound. Later versions continued to reinterpret the name: Campbell White’s Special Guest treatment emphasized spectral scale, while Anna Pavleeva’s Secret Lair version approached the card through a more human and illustrative emotional register.

Grief’s visual history accumulated quickly because the card became famous quickly. Within a few years, it had regular, borderless, retro-frame, Special Guest, digital, and Secret Lair objects. That abundance reflects modern Magic’s treatment system, but it also records the card’s rapid passage from preview-season mythic to format villain to banned historical artifact.

X · The cycle

The Incarnation cycle

The five Modern Horizons 2 Incarnations shared a frame, a pitch cost, and an emergency purpose, but competitive play separated them into different careers.

White

Solitude

Free creature exile with lifelink; a defining control and blink card, later banned in Historic.

Blue

Subtlety

A tempo answer to creatures and planeswalkers that trades permanence for timing.

Black

Grief

Chosen discard before development; uniquely suited to turn-one recursion.

Red

Fury

Divided creature and planeswalker damage; banned in Modern before Grief.

Green

Endurance

Graveyard reset, surprise blocker, and a lasting Eternal-format safety valve.

The cycle changed the meaning of holding up interaction. A tapped-out player could still threaten removal, graveyard disruption, hand attack, or tempo. The price was visible in card economy, but the opponent could no longer infer safety from mana alone.

Grief became the most emotionally charged member because it acted before hidden information became public action. Solitude answered a creature the opponent had chosen to cast. Endurance answered a graveyard that had been built. Grief answered the intention itself.

Modern Horizons 2 · 2021

Five free spells wearing creature frames

The cycle was designed as emergency interaction paid for with a card rather than mana. Grief demonstrated that a creature frame creates opportunities no instant could offer.

Its legacy is therefore larger than one ban: it changed how players evaluate every enters-the-battlefield creature with an alternate cost.

XI · Testimony

What players remember

Players remember Grief in sequences rather than isolated casts.

Keep seven. Reveal it. Lose the answer. Lose the backup answer. Face a menace creature before playing a land.
The compressed experience that made “getting Griefed” its own phrase
The card was rarely unbeatable by itself. The frustration came from discovering that the game you kept for had already been replaced.
Modern and Legacy tournament memory, 2021–2024

Supporters of the card remember something else: difficult mulligan decisions, sequencing with the evoke trigger, choosing the correct card from an opponent’s hand, and building a deck with enough black cards to make the pitch cost reliable. Rakdos Evoke was not a slot machine that played itself. Its best pilots converted an explosive mechanic into a disciplined midrange plan.

Both memories are true. Grief rewarded technical play and produced non-games. It was an elegant use of creature rules and a source of repetitive opening misery. It won a Pro Tour without dominating every statistical measure, then remained objectionable enough to be banned after its most famous companion had already left.

That contradiction is why the card belongs in the archive. Grief was not merely powerful. It exposed the difference between a format being competitively solvable and a format producing games people wished to play.
