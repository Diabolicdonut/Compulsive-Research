I · Opening hand

The One-Drop That Turned Mana into Combat Damage

Original object · Conflux

Noble Hierarch

Creature — Human Druid

Exalted (Whenever a creature you control attacks alone, that creature gets +1/+1 until end of turn.) {T}: Add {G}, {W}, or {U}.

Original setConflux · 2009
Mana cost{G}
Mana value1.0
Power / toughness0 / 1

Noble Hierarch looks smaller than the decks built around it. It is a 0/1 that cannot attack profitably, produces only three colors of mana, and dies to nearly every removal spell ever aimed at a creature. Yet the card became one of the most influential one-drops of the modern era because neither half of its text becomes obsolete when the other half is no longer needed.

On the first turns, the Hierarch is acceleration. It turns a one-land opening into a credible turn-two three-drop and lets decks curve into threats while holding interaction. Once mana is plentiful, exalted changes the card’s job. A lone attacker receives +1/+1, so the supposed mana source becomes a permanent combat trick that stacks with every additional exalted creature.

That compression explains its movement through Extended Zoo, Bant, Birthing Pod, Infect, Humans, and creature-toolbox strategies. Those decks did not merely need a green mana creature. They needed a one-drop that fixed awkward colors, enabled an early play, and remained meaningful when the game narrowed to one protected attacker.

This article follows the card from Conflux development through its major tournament homes, then turns the history into practical guidance: which land to fetch before casting it, when to spend it for mana, when to leave it untapped, how exalted changes racing arithmetic, and why opponents often have only one turn to remove it efficiently.

II · Conflux

Bant found its perfect one-drop

Noble Hierarch debuted in Conflux in February 2009, returning to the exalted mechanic introduced in Shards of Alara. An official Conflux design feature records that the card began much more generically: a three-mana common that tapped for any color. Development compressed it to one mana, restricted the output to green, white, and blue, and attached the result to Bant’s established exalted identity.

That restriction matters. The card is not simply Birds of Paradise with a bonus ability. It asks the deck to live primarily in Bant colors or to use other lands for black and red. In exchange, it offers combat relevance that Birds lacks. The trade made the card both narrower and more strategically expressive.

Mark Zug’s original illustration presents a Bant hierarch as a ceremonial figure rather than a battlefield aggressor. The posture is upright and still, framed by the ordered architecture and luminous sigils associated with Bant. The image matches the mechanics: authority is exercised indirectly, through the champion who attacks alone.

Because it was printed at rare, the card also announced that a mana creature could be a premium constructed object rather than merely a functional common. Its tournament life justified that confidence almost immediately.

Mark Zug’s original Conflux artworkThe illustration organizes the figure through a readable silhouette, layered depth, and directional light. Foreground texture and background atmosphere keep the subject legible at card size while still suggesting a larger world beyond the frame.
III · Two jobs

A mana ability and a combat trigger share one body

The activated ability is a mana ability. It does not use the stack, so an opponent cannot respond after the Hierarch is tapped and before the mana appears. The opponent must remove it before the controller receives priority to use it, or interact with the spell that the mana helps cast.

Exalted triggers whenever a creature controlled by the Hierarch’s controller attacks alone. “Alone” means exactly one creature was declared as an attacker. A second creature entering the battlefield attacking does not undo the trigger, while declaring two attackers prevents it from triggering at all.

Multiple exalted abilities trigger separately and stack. Two Hierarchs make one attacker +2/+2; a board containing other exalted permanents can turn a small evasive creature into a serious clock without committing additional bodies to combat.

The trigger does not require Noble Hierarch to be untapped. The same copy can produce mana before combat and still grant exalted later that turn, provided it remains on the battlefield when attackers are declared.

IV · Role compression

The mana is only half the card

Traditional mana creatures create a familiar tension: they are excellent before the deck reaches its expensive spells and poor after it has drawn enough lands. Noble Hierarch reduces that decline. A late copy may still be modest, but it increases damage immediately without entering combat or risking itself in a block.

The card also changes the value of attacking with one creature. Infect decks can concentrate pump spells on Glistener Elf or Blighted Agent; Humans can send a single evasive Mantis Rider; Bant decks can attack with a protected Knight of the Reliquary. In each case, exalted rewards the line the deck already wants.

That extra point frequently represents more than one damage. It can move a creature outside burn range, win combat against an equal body, add an infect counter, or shorten the opponent’s clock by an entire turn. A mana creature that changes those thresholds is not merely producing a resource; it is altering the combat system.

The body’s fragility remains the balancing mechanism. A deck that keeps a one-land hand because Hierarch supplies the second mana source accepts the risk that a cheap removal spell will function as land destruction.

V · Tournament scene

From Grove Zoo to Pod, Humans, and Nadu

The first great stage

Brian Kibler’s winning Grove Zoo list at Pro Tour Austin 2009 used Noble Hierarch to accelerate Knight of the Reliquary, fix a demanding mana base, and add exalted damage to Wild Nacatl or Tarmogoyf attacks. The deck demonstrated the central promise: the one-drop could belong in an aggressive shell without becoming a dead draw when acceleration mattered less.

Toolbox years

Modern Birthing Pod lists used Hierarch as both a turn-one accelerator and a creature that could be sacrificed up the Pod chain. Jacob Wilson’s runner-up list from Pro Tour Born of the Gods shows how naturally it fit a deck whose individual creatures needed to perform several jobs.

Tribal mana without the creature-type requirement

Five-color Humans adopted Noble Hierarch despite the card not being a Human payoff itself. It cast multicolored creatures through awkward early sequences, while exalted made a single Mantis Rider or Champion of the Parish more difficult to race. Andrea Mengucci’s 2018 Pro Tour list captures the archetype at its breakout moment.

The toolbox returns

Simon Nielsen’s winning Nadu deck at Pro Tour Modern Horizons 3 used only one Noble Hierarch, searchable and functional inside a dense creature engine. The singleton illustrates the card’s longevity: even when newer one-drops exist, the exact combination of Bant mana, creature type, and exalted can remain worth a specialized slot.

VI · Construction

Three colors, one attacker, no wasted slot

Noble Hierarch is strongest when all three mana colors matter. A nominally green-white deck gains less from the blue symbol than Bant, Humans, or Infect, while a four-color deck must still solve the black or red portion elsewhere. The first question is therefore not “Do I want acceleration?” but “How often does this exact mana map unlock my hand?”

The second question is whether the deck can exploit a lone attacker. Evasion, infect, large single threats, and attack triggers all magnify exalted. Token decks and go-wide strategies may prefer mana creatures whose late-game text supports multiple attackers instead.

Land counts can fall when four Hierarchs are present, but only within limits. Hands that require the creature to survive are much riskier than hands where it merely improves the curve. Successful lists generally pair Hierarch with enough real lands or redundant accelerants that one removal spell does not end the game.

Creature-based engines gain another benefit: Hierarch can be found by creature tutors, sacrificed for value, returned from the graveyard, or counted for effects that care about creatures. That flexibility explains why it appears in shells that would never register an artifact mana source with the same text.

VII · Sequencing

The turn-one land decides turn three

A turn-one Hierarch begins with the land choice. Fetching a basic Forest protects against nonbasic hate but may leave white or blue spells stranded if the Hierarch dies. Fetching a dual land increases color access but can expose the deck to cards such as Wasteland or Blood Moon. The correct land is the one that leaves the hand functional after the creature is removed.

On turn two, decide whether the mana creature is an accelerant, an exalted source, or both. Tapping it before combat does not turn off exalted, but spending every mana before attacking can reveal that no protection spell remains. Sometimes the best use is to attack first, preserve uncertainty, and cast the three-drop afterward.

When several creatures could attack, count damage over multiple turns rather than maximizing the current combat. Sending one larger evasive creature may deal less now but preserve blockers and avoid exposing a second creature to a sweeper or favorable block.

Against open removal, ask which exchange is worse: losing the Hierarch before it produces mana, or losing the intended attacker after committing to a lone attack. The answer changes with the hand, and the card’s strength comes partly from forcing that decision every turn.

VIII · Counterplay

Kill the accelerator before it becomes a pump spell

The cleanest answer is removal before the Hierarch’s controller untaps. At that point, a one-mana removal spell may deny both a mana and the planned curve. After the accelerated threat has resolved, killing the Hierarch still matters, but the tempo exchange has already changed.

Do not ignore exalted because the battlefield contains larger creatures. A 0/1 can be the difference between a profitable block and a losing one, or between nine and ten poison counters. Recalculate combat before choosing targets.

Mass one-damage effects are particularly efficient because Hierarch decks often contain several small utility creatures. Effects that punish creature types can also catch it as a Human or Druid, though tribal decks may force difficult naming choices.

When removal is scarce, attacking the mana base can be better. The card produces no black or red, and many Hierarch decks rely on nonbasic lands to complete their colors. Removing the creature and the wrong land in sequence can collapse a hand that looked perfectly fixed.

Finally, pressure can invalidate the exalted plan. A player forced to block with the intended attacker no longer receives value from sending one creature alone. Wide battlefields and racing clocks can make the Hierarch’s combat text much less impressive.

IX · Format migration

Each format asks for a different half

In Extended and early Modern, Noble Hierarch was primarily a rate card: one mana, three colors, and a clean jump from one to three. Zoo and Birthing Pod exploited the acceleration while accepting its fragility.

Legacy emphasized the tension between acceleration and hostile mana interaction. Hierarch could power out a threat, but Wasteland, cheap removal, and efficient countermagic punished hands built too heavily around it. Bant and Infect succeeded when the card’s exalted half justified that risk.

Modern Infect made exalted almost a second pump spell. Because poison is counted separately and ends the game at ten, one additional power changes the clock sharply. Modern Humans instead valued the combination of fixing and one-attacker pressure in a deck full of disruptive creatures.

Commander usually treats the card as a specialized Bant accelerator rather than a universal staple. Its exalted trigger scales poorly when several opponents must be defeated, but Equipment, Voltron, and exalted commanders can still convert the small body into reliable damage.

X · Art and memory

The quiet creature behind louder wins

Noble Hierarch rarely delivered the memorable final blow itself. The card appeared one step earlier: it made the turn-two threat possible, fixed the third color, or supplied the exalted point that changed combat. That supporting role helped it become an expert player’s icon rather than a conventional marquee creature.

Mark Zug’s original art reinforces that identity. The central figure does not charge. She stands within Bant’s ceremonial geometry, directing power toward another champion. The image turns the rules text into a social structure: the hierarch’s authority is visible through the creature chosen to fight alone.

The card’s enduring reputation also comes from how easy it is to underestimate. Players remember losing to the large creature, then discover on review that the one-drop shaped every turn that led to the attack.

XI · Design echo

The mirror moved to Jund

Ignoble Hierarch is the clearest later acknowledgment of the original design: the same cost, body, exalted ability, and three-color mana pattern moved from Bant to black-red-green.

A comparative echo

Ignoble Hierarch

Modern Horizons 2 preserved the role compression while changing the decks and threats that could use it.

The mirrored card demonstrates how complete the original template was. R&D did not need to add another reward; changing the color trio was enough to create a new constructed tool.

XII · Decision point

Mana now or damage later?

Suppose the battlefield contains Noble Hierarch and a 2/2 evasive creature, while the hand contains a three-mana threat and a one-mana protection spell. Tapping Hierarch and the available land before combat casts the threat, but leaves the attacker exposed. Attacking first presents three damage and keeps the protection spell credible.

The correct line depends on the opponent’s resources. Against sorcery-speed interaction, using the mana immediately may be safe. Against instant removal, the protected exalted attack may preserve both tempo and the best threat.

A second layer appears when the opponent can remove Hierarch in combat. Because exalted has already triggered, killing the Hierarch after attackers are declared does not remove the bonus from the stack. If the opponent wants to prevent the trigger entirely, the removal must happen before the attack declaration.

These small priority and sequencing details explain why the card rewards practice. Its text is short, but the value is distributed across the whole turn.

XIII · Legacy

A one-drop that compresses roles

Noble Hierarch established a durable standard for premium mana creatures. Efficiency alone was not enough; the best versions would accelerate early and contribute a different resource later.

Later cards explored the model in different directions. Some added graveyard utility, protection, tribal restrictions, or legendary-spell clauses. Few matched the original’s clean union of fixing and combat arithmetic.

The result is a card whose influence is visible whenever deck builders ask whether a mana creature can remain valuable after the mana is no longer needed. Noble Hierarch answered yes by attacking through someone else.
