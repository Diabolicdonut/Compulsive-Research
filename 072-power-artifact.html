I · The central bargain

The Enchantment That Broke Mana Costs

Power Artifact turns the generic mana in artifact activation costs into a combo equation. Enchant Basalt Monolith: its three-mana untap cost drops to one, while tapping the Monolith still produces three colorless.

The Aura reduces the generic portion of the enchanted artifact's activated-ability costs by {2}, not below one mana when a mana payment is present. It does not discount casting costs, colored mana, tap costs or mana abilities that already cost zero.

A story from the table

The Monolith paid three and untapped for one

My Basalt Monolith had been in play since the previous turn when I tapped two Islands to cast Power Artifact on it. The Aura resolved. I tapped Monolith for three colorless mana, spent one to activate its discounted untap ability, and kept two floating. I untapped Monolith and tapped it for three more. Repeating that exchange could build as much colorless mana as I needed, but Stroke of Genius would still require blue mana from another source. I watched my opponent's untapped Underground Sea: removing the Aura at the right moment could leave the Monolith unable to continue.

Strategic legacy

Why Power Artifact matters

Power Artifact reduces an artifact’s activated costs by two mana, a modest-looking discount that becomes infinite mana when attached to the right monolith.

How to use it

Assemble Power Artifact with Basalt Monolith or Grim Monolith, protect the aura from removal, and include a payoff that converts unlimited colorless mana into a win.

How to fight it

Remove the enchanted artifact or the aura in response to the combo, counter either half, or use effects that stop activated abilities. Graveyard recursion means one answer may not be enough.

II · Origin

The original printing and its bargain

Douglas Shuler illustrated Power Artifact for Antiquities in March 1994. The combination with Basalt Monolith became a classic cost-reduction example; Wizards later specifically removed power-level errata restricting Basalt Monolith's self-untap in July 2006.

VII · Failure modes

When the famous card is bad

Reputation can hide failure modes. Power Artifact is not powerful merely because history remembers it; it is powerful only when the game supplies the conditions its exchange requires.

The first failure is structural: the deck needs the correct artifact, ways to find and protect both pieces, and a payoff for unlimited or greatly increased mana. Draws that ignore this constraint often contain the famous card but cannot use it without damaging their own development. That is why experienced deck builders count colored sources, expendable permanents, targets, protection, and timing windows rather than treating the card as a free upgrade.

Power Artifact is also matchup-dependent. A card that dominates the resource it was built to attack can look ordinary when the opponent has redundant mana, irrelevant creatures, a faster combo, or answers that trade cleanly. Sideboarding should therefore preserve it for games where its specific exchange remains profitable.

Fame creates another trap: the pilot may chase the celebrated Power Artifact line instead of taking a smaller winning exchange. The correct play can be to produce mana, decline an activation, trade for one card, or hold the threat in reserve; historical identity does not make every use mandatory.

Finally, every historical card meets newer efficiency. Training Grounds, Biomancer's Familiar, Zirda, and numerous cost-reduction combo pieces inherit the same mathematical danger can make the original look clumsy in a vacuum. Yet comparison should ask what was lost as well as what was improved. Newer cards may be safer, narrower, more expensive, less symmetrical, or unable to reproduce the precise interactions that made the original distinctive. Understanding the weaknesses of Power Artifact clarifies its real contribution better than uncritical celebration.

VIII · Counterplay

How the other side learned to fight back

A card becomes a format fixture only after opponents learn how to fight it. The most reliable answers to Power Artifact include removing the artifact in response to the Aura, countering either half, graveyard hate against recursion, and taxing activated abilities.

Defeating Power Artifact means identifying which layer actually matters. Remove the enchanted artifact or the aura in response to the combo, counter either half, or use effects that stop activated abilities. Graveyard recursion means one answer may not be enough. In some games the permanent is the target; in others, attacking the mana, graveyard, creature density, or payoff leaves the namesake card stranded.

A clock can be as important as a direct answer. Pressure forces the Power Artifact deck to use its resource before the ideal window, shortening the time available to assemble protection or repeat the exchange that makes the card powerful.

That interaction produced a recurring metagame cycle around Power Artifact: successful lists forced opponents to reserve answers, pilots adapted with protection or alternate plans, and the card shifted between centerpiece, specialist, and historical benchmark.

X · Format migration

One text box, several eras

The long history of Power Artifact is not a straight line of power. It is a sequence of card pools discovering different portions of the same text.

Power Artifact's role changed as later sets supplied faster threats, cleaner answers, tutors, and new engines. Its identity is inseparable from monolith combo, making it an early example of a cost-reduction card whose wording accidentally crosses from efficiency into infinity. The same text could therefore move from centerpiece to support card without losing its importance as the original expression of the idea.

That migration is visible in Training Grounds, Biomancer's Familiar, Zirda, and numerous cost-reduction combo pieces inherit the same mathematical danger. Each descendant keeps one part of the original proposition while changing another variable—mana cost, symmetry, card type, speed, vulnerability, or scale. Comparing them reveals what designers believed had to be preserved and what had to be restrained. The newer card may be cleaner, but the older one often remains stranger because it was printed before its category had settled into modern limits.

Different communities preserve different versions of Power Artifact. Players of its Antiquities era remember the original exchange; Eternal and historical formats remember the archetypes that refined it; Commander, Cube, and collectors preserve the table ritual, artwork, and scarcity surrounding the object.

XI · Modern reading

How to evaluate it now

Evaluating Power Artifact today requires separating three questions that are often collapsed: Is it legal? Is it competitively efficient? Is it historically representative?

Legality and accessibility answer only part of the modern question. Whether Power Artifact is available in a format, restricted by policy, or difficult to acquire does not erase the environment that taught players to value its text; historical evaluation must preserve both the rules object and the games built around it.

By current efficiency standards, Power Artifact must compete with later cards that offer safer mana, immediate value, or broader interaction. Its relevance rises whenever a new partner restores the original exchange and falls when opponents can answer it without sacrificing tempo.

Historical representation asks a different question: does the card explain an era, an archetype, or a design idea better than its successors? For Power Artifact, the answer lies in the lesson that reducing a repeatable cost is often more explosive than increasing a one-time output. Even where a later card is stronger, the original may show the bargain in a purer and more revealing form.

A present-day player can approach Power Artifact as a permanent for historical play, a build-around where legal, a Commander or Cube choice, a collectible printing, and a design case study from Antiquities. Those uses reinforce rather than replace one another.

XII · Design and memory

Why the idea survived

The final measure of Power Artifact is not whether every modern deck wants it. It is whether later Magic still thinks in concepts the card helped establish.

Its design legacy is that reducing a repeatable cost is often more explosive than increasing a one-time output. Once players internalize that lesson, they recognize it everywhere: in new card previews, in deck-building heuristics, and in the shorthand used to describe an unfamiliar effect. A card becomes historically important when its name can stand in for an entire family of decisions.

Its cultural memory is equally concrete: the quiet moment when two innocuous-looking permanents change the game from finite arithmetic to an arbitrarily large number. These details matter because players remember games through physical rituals—counters moved, lands left untapped, cards selected at random, life totals rewritten, or a single permanent placed conspicuously beside the mana base. The rules create the decision, but the table gesture preserves it.

That combination of strategic lesson and sensory memory is why the card belongs in an archive rather than merely a database. Oracle text can describe what Power Artifact does. A historical essay must also preserve what the table felt like once both players understood what it could do.
